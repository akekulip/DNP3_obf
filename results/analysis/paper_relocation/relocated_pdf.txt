Abstract—Device ﬁngerprinting lets adversaries in industrial
control systems (ICSs) learn which devices and operations they
face before an attack. Existing traﬃc obfuscation pads, splits and
delays packets over encrypted channels. However, ICS networks
still carry plain-text protocols such as DNP3, where ﬁller and
dummy packets are visible, and ICS ﬁngerprinting relies on the
timing between diﬀerent types of packets, e.g., the cross-layer
response time (CLRT) between a TCP acknowledgment and the
response. In this paper, we present NOMAD, an in-network
traﬃc obfuscation mechanism that uses the syntax of control
units to apply a diﬀerent strategy to each type of operation on
a programmable switch. NOMAD replaces the CLRT of reads
with a conﬁgured value, holds control commands inside the
switch, and splits the control units of every response into one
ﬁxed layout, without changing the endpoints or the meaning of
any control unit. We implement NOMAD on an Intel Toﬁno1 switch and evaluate it with 98,816 DNP3 transactions. The
CLRT at the master stays within 10–16 𝜇s of the conﬁgured
value at the median, and all responses carry 61 bytes and remain
conformant to IEEE 1815. Against the CLRT classiﬁer of Formby
et al., NOMAD’s eﬀect is limited on our testbed: trained before
deployment, the classiﬁer falls to chance, as it also does under a
constant delay of 1 ms, and retrained on the obfuscated traﬃc
it learns about as much as without NOMAD. All transactions
complete within 53 ms without retransmission.

I. INTRODUCTION
Device ﬁngerprinting is an essential step in cyber reconnaissance, allowing adversaries to reveal unique features of target
networks and design eﬀective attack strategies. These techniques are becoming increasingly critical in industrial control
systems (ICSs) such as power grids, where adversaries often
use IP-based control networks and computing devices within
as entry points to initiate physical damage. Consequently,
ﬁngerprinting shifts the focus from visited websites and user
biometric behavior to device models and types of control
operations that are critical to ICS attacks. In the 2015 attack
that disrupted Ukrainian power grids and the Stuxnet attack
that disrupted Iranian nuclear power facilities, it is widely
believed that adversaries stay in their systems for at least 6
months to perform cyber reconnaissance [1], [2].
To disrupt device ﬁngerprinting, many studies present network traﬃc obfuscation [3], [4]. Device ﬁngerprinting targeting general computing environments typically relies on
network-level features such as packet size and/or inter-packet

latency observed from communication patterns [5]. Consequently, existing traﬃc obfuscation focuses on (i) padding and
splitting network packets, which hide or change the distribution
of network packet sizes [3], and (ii) delaying network packets and adding dummy ones, which disrupt the inter-packet
timing pattern [4], [6]. Because manipulating communication
networks can introduce runtime overhead, recent work has
begun oﬄoading traﬃc obfuscation to programmable network
switches, using specialized ASIC to change communication
patterns at much higher line rates than CPUs [7]–[9].
Unfortunately, these methods cannot be directly applied to
ICS environments for two main reasons. First, existing traﬃc
obfuscation is performed over encrypted channels, which are
necessary to mix the obfuscated and normal traﬃc. ICS networks, on the other hand, still rely on plain-text communications traﬃc due to a wide range of legacy devices, despite ICS
network protocols providing security features [10]. Directly
padding or adding dummy packets can easily reveal obfuscated
packets, exposing the underlying traﬃc pattern to adversaries.
Second, device ﬁngerprinting methods on ICS devices rely
on diﬀerent features from the ones in general computing
environments. Instead of using network layer knowledge, they
attempt to use behavior on physical devices to reveal device
model or types of operations. For example, work in [11] uses
cross-layer response time (CLRT), determined by the latency
between the TCP acknowledge packets (ACK) and the actual
response from the same device to estimate execution time
there. Since each vendor may choose diﬀerent materials or
algorithms to implement the same control functionality, CLRT
becomes an eﬀective feature for ICS device ﬁngerprinting.
In this paper, we present NOMAD, an in-network trafﬁc obfuscation mechanism against ICS device ﬁngerprinting,
even with plain-text communication. Consequently, adversaries cannot conduct eﬀective reconnaissance using sidechannel knowledge to reveal target systems’ vulnerabilities
(e.g., outdated ﬁrmware or vulnerable device models) from
network communications. Our solution draws on a key insight:
to carry control units (e.g., measurements and commands), ICS
network protocols map them to numerical indices, with syntax
varying across control operations. Instead of applying the
same obfuscation strategy to each network packet (as in many
existing solutions), we use programmable network switches to

extract operation-related knowledge from network traﬃc and
apply diﬀerent strategies by splitting, padding, and scheduling
control units while maintaining protocol compliance. NOMAD
realizes multiple advancements with small runtime overhead:
(i) provide ﬂexible obfuscation (e.g., normalize TCP ACK and
application response in two diﬀerent patterns); (ii) preserve
communication with legitimate end devices; and (iii) require
no reliance on de-obfuscation hardware and encryption over
ICS traﬃc.
We realize NOMAD on a single Intel Toﬁno-1 programmable switch placed in front of an outstation, and we
instantiate three example strategies on the DNP3 protocol.
First, the switch holds the TCP acknowledgment and the
DNP3 response of a READ or SELECT and releases them
at deadlines computed from the request, so that the CLRT
a passive observer measures becomes a value the operator
conﬁgures. Second, it holds each OPERATE command for
a conﬁgured interval before delivering it, so that the timing
of a control operation no longer follows the device. Third, it
splits the control units of every response into a ﬁxed layout
of index ranges and transport frames, so that all responses
have the same size while carrying the same measurements
and status. None of these strategies changes the master, the
outstation, or the meaning of a control unit. We evaluate
NOMAD on a hardware testbed, with OpenDNP3 master and
outstation endpoints behind the switch, over 98,816 DNP3
transactions under four obfuscation conﬁgurations and eight
timing settings. Speciﬁcally, NOMAD makes the following
contributions:

II. BACKGROUND
A. ICS Network Communications
Like many ICSs, power grids comprise cyber and physical
infrastructures. Physically, substations (buses) are linked by
electrical lines into a transmission or distribution network.
The cyber infrastructure connects ﬁeld devices, e.g., IEDs
(intelligent electronic devices), PLCs (programmable logic
controllers), and legacy sensors and breakers, to a control
center over an IP-based control network. The control center
uses supervisory control and data acquisition (or a similar
setup) to periodically collect measurements (voltage, current,
power consumption) and issue commands to maintain stable,
safe physical processes.
ICS network protocols, e.g., DNP3 and Modbus, are designed mainly to carry control units, e.g., measurements or
control commands, to accomplish these functions. The syntax
of control units varies across operational types. For example,
the DNP3 protocol speciﬁes primitive types, e.g., ﬂoating
points, to represent measurements and composite types, e.g.,
control relay output block (CROB), to represent commands
applied to circuit breakers. The protocol further uses numerical
indices to organize various control units in a single network
packet. The protocol-conforming devices, e.g., IEDs and PLCs,
store a local conﬁguration ﬁle, to look up the meaning of a
control unit based on its indices.
For example, Table I lists the analog input points that an
SEL protective relay exposes over DNP3. Each measurement,
from the three phase currents to the power factor, travels only
as an index between 0 and 20, and the master interprets a
value through the same conﬁguration. A DNP3 request names
the points it wants by an object group, a qualiﬁer, and a range
of indices, and the response returns the values under object
headers that name the indices they carry [10]. Consequently,
the same set of control units admits more than one valid layout,
e.g., one range of indices or several consecutive ranges, and
a response can change its size without changing what it says
(Section V).

mechanical components to implement the same control operations, adversaries can use side-channel knowledge from control

vulnerable device models or ﬁrmware versions, enabling eﬀective attacks. For example, studies in [11] use two proprietary
features for device ﬁngerprinting, as shown in Fig. 1. Crosslayer response time (CLRT), calculated by the latency between
a TCP ACK packet and the corresponding application layer
response, estimates the time used by speciﬁc IEDs to retrieve
measurements. Physical Response Time (PRT), calculated as

C. Network Switch with Programmable Network Dataplane
Network switches with programmable network data plane
enabled by P4 draw signiﬁcant attention to host in-network
security analytics. P4 stands for Programming Protocolindependent Packet Processors and is the de facto data-plane
programming language enable customized packet-processing
functions [12]. As shown in Fig. 2, Portable Switch Architecture (PSA) provides reference infrastructure for a programmable network data plane. It can feature up to four ingress
and four egress pipelines [13], each composed of a parser,
a deparser, and a sequence of match/action units (MAUs).
MAUs include various storage units (e.g., SRAM, TCAM) to
store network knowledge and arithmetic logic units (ALUs)
to enable the following features directly in network devices:
customized packet parsing across all network layers, line-rate
analysis, and integrated monitoring and traﬃc engineering.
However, realizing these beneﬁts requires meeting with strict
hardware limits, e.g., roughly 120 MB of SRAM, 10 MB of
TCAM, and ALUs that do not support ﬂoating-point operations, multiplication, or division.

B. Design Goals
Before describing NOMAD’s design goal, we use a sample network trace collected from IEDs from two diﬀerent
vendors, i.e., the Schweitzer Engineering Laboratories (SEL)
751A [16] and the Schneider Electric (SE) ION7550 power
meter [17], to demonstrate unique characteristics in ICS device
ﬁngerprinting. First, despite establishing security extensions in
common ICS network protocols, plain-text communication is
still prevalent in power grids and other ICSs. We have developed a web crawler that examined public product manuals,
datasheets, and pages from ﬁve IED vendors, namely, SEL,
SE, Rockwell Automation, GE Vernova, and Siemens. With a
stratiﬁed sample of 99 catalogued entries, 32 were identiﬁed
as having DNP3 support, and these documents established
encryption for six entries, and one had DNP3 authentication.
However, it is also established that OT asset owners and
operators do not use these security measures even if they
exist, with numerous reasons hindering their adoption [18].
The crawler methodology and detailed results are presented in
Appendix A.

Obfuscation over Plain-text. Despite ICS network protocols are gradually enhanced with security components,
plain-text communication still prevails in ICS due to

∙
Flexible Obfuscation. Features from diﬀerent types of

∙

C. Components and Workﬂows
As shown in Fig. 5, NOMAD includes two components:
(i) Obfuscation infrastructure that uses P4-enabled network
data planes to apply ﬂexible obfuscation strategies and (ii)
obfuscation objectives/constraints determining security properties in various scenarios while observing ICS communication
requirements. When a network packet traverses a gateway
network device, we use a customized parser to extract network
attributes and control-related knowledge, then categorize packets and organize the carried control units accordingly in the
switch’s local storage. Using the control-unit syntax, including
sizes and indices, we can split, pad, and schedule control units
according to diﬀerent operations while maintaining protocol
compliance. While obfuscation infrastructures provide an innetwork hardware foundation, we use switches’ control plane
to realize various obfuscation objectives under constraints
for diverse security applications, e.g., neutralize, mimicry
communication patterns, and/or maximize diﬀerences between
obfuscated and original patterns.
IV. O BFUSCATION INFRASTRUCTURE
To apply a diﬀerent strategy to each type of operation,
NOMAD must know, for every packet that crosses the switch,
which transaction it belongs to and which control units it
carries, and it must be able to hold a packet and release it
later without a host in the loop. As shown in Fig. 5, packets
entering the switch pass three modules: a classiﬁer, which
extracts control units and binds packets into transactions; a
holder, which keeps packets until the strategy releases them;
and a rewriter, which changes the layout of responses while
keeping both TCP endpoints consistent. In this section, we
describe each module by its function and its state, and we
leave the policies they apply to Section V.
A. Classiﬁer
The classiﬁer parses every TCP segment between the master
and the outstation down to the DNP3 application layer. It extracts the function code of each request, e.g., READ, SELECT
and OPERATE, and the object group, variation, qualiﬁer and
index range of each object header (Section II). Based on these
ﬁelds and the TCP connection, we bind the request, its TCP
acknowledgment and its response into one transaction, and
we record the arrival time 𝑡0 of the request, from which the
strategies of Section V compute their deadlines. Requests leave
the classiﬁer unchanged. Consequently, the classiﬁcation of a
packet adds no byte to the request a device receives.

B. Holder
On our switch, the data plane cannot pause a queue, and a
packet cannot wait inside a match/action stage. We therefore
hold a packet in a queue of the traﬃc manager and decide
its release with blocker tokens, i.e., small internal packets that
occupy a queue of higher priority and keep the scheduler from
serving the held packet. A token re-enters the switch through
recirculation after each pass, so the holder checks the clock
once per pass without any host involvement. The holder oﬀers
three operations on a packet 𝑝 whose release deadline 𝑑𝑝 the
strategy sets, possibly after 𝑝 arrives; e.g., Strategy 1 ﬁxes
an acknowledgment’s deadline only once the response of its
transaction has been seen, and a response’s deadline only once
the acknowledgment has been released:
∙ hold(𝑝, 𝑑𝑝 ): enqueue 𝑝 in a hold queue 𝑄𝐿 and inject a
blocker token 𝑏𝑝 , which carries 𝑑𝑝 , into a queue 𝑄𝐻 that
the scheduler serves with strict priority over 𝑄𝐿 .
∙ pass(𝑏𝑝 ): on each pass of 𝑏𝑝 at time 𝜏, recirculate 𝑏𝑝 into
𝑄𝐻 if 𝜏 < 𝑑𝑝 , and drop it otherwise.
∙ release(𝑝): the scheduler serves 𝑄𝐿 , and therefore 𝑝, only
once 𝑄𝐻 is empty.

separate pairs of queues, so a held packet of one class never
waits behind the token of another. Each queue pair holds
at most one packet at a time, because DNP3 allows one
outstanding request per connection and NOMAD assigns one
queue pair per class of packet. Let 𝛿 denote the duration of
one recirculation pass. Because the last token of 𝑝 is dropped

C. Rewriter
The rewriter applies the response layouts of Section V as
a response leaves the switch toward the master. Because a reencoded response is longer than the original, the TCP byte
stream seen by the master no longer matches the one the
outstation sent. Let Δ denote the number of bytes by which
the rewriter has lengthened the responses of a connection so
far. To keep both endpoints consistent, we in eﬀect add Δ
to the sequence numbers of the outstation’s segments toward
the master and subtract it from the acknowledgment numbers
of the master’s segments toward the outstation. The rewriter
changes a response only when its mapping state commits
the new layout, so a ﬂow without a conﬁgured layout is
never lengthened without a matching translation, and the TCP
checksum of every rewritten frame is updated accordingly.

V. O BFUSCATION STRATEGIES
NOMAD applies a strategy per type of operation rather than
per packet. In this section, we present three example strategies,
which together cover the timing and size features of the two
transactions in Fig. 1: a timing replacement for READ and
SELECT, a hold for OPERATE, and a response layout that
splits control units. Each strategy is a policy installed by the
control plane on the infrastructure of Section IV.

C. Strategy 3: Splitting Control Units
Responses of diﬀerent operations diﬀer in size, and the
size alone can reveal the operation. In our testbed, a READ
response carries 49 bytes of TCP payload and a SELECT or
OPERATE response carries 37 bytes. Padding such a response
with ﬁller would either break the DNP3 syntax or expose the
ﬁller to an observer who parses the plain text. We, on the
other hand, change the layout of the control units the response
already carries with the index syntax of Section II.
READ responses. In our testbed, the native READ response
returns 23 binary output status points (object group 10, variation 2), indices 0 to 22, in one object header. We re-encode the
same points as three consecutive index ranges, 0–5 and 6–9
with one-byte start and stop indices and 10–22 with two-byte
indices. Every value appears once, in the same order, and the
response grows to 61 bytes.
SELECT and OPERATE responses. The 22 application
bytes of the native response are split as 15, 6 and 1 bytes across
three DNP3 transport frames of 28, 19 and 14 bytes, which
travel in one TCP segment of 61 bytes. The master’s DNP3
stack reassembles the three frames into the original application
fragment.
In both cases, a normalized response carries 61 bytes, the
master receives the same control units in the same order,
and every frame keeps valid CRCs and transport sequence
numbers. Requests stay as the master sent them; NOMAD
normalizes the responses only.

The strategies of Section V take their parameters from the

obfuscation objective states what the observer should see; a
constraint states what the control network and the switch must
still guarantee. In this section, we describe both and the rule
NOMAD uses to turn them into deadlines.
Objectives. The operator states three targets: the CLRT
the master should observe, 𝐷; the hold applied to every

all outstations behind the switch that acknowledge requests
separately, so that such devices present the same timing and
size to the master-facing link.

A. Testbed and Methodology
Testbed. We run NOMAD on an Intel Toﬁno-1 switch
with SDE 9.13.2, as three P4 programs (Appendix B). The
DNP3 master and outstation are OpenDNP3 [19] endpoints
in two network namespaces of one server, connected through
the same switch port, so every packet between them crosses
the switch, and the outstation answers a request after a ﬁxed
5 ms application delay. We capture every packet on the server’s
switch-facing interface with microsecond timestamps, once
as the endpoints send it and once as the switch delivers it;
the master-facing observation of Section III is the delivered
stream.
Conﬁgurations. We compare four conﬁgurations on the
same compiled image: No obfuscation, Timing (Strategies 1
and 2), Size (Strategy 3) and Timing+Size (all three). We vary
the conﬁgured CLRT 𝐷 over 5, 10, 15 and 20 ms with 𝐽 =
2.4 ms, and the OPERATE hold 𝐽 over 5, 10, 15 and 20 ms
with 𝐷 = 10 ms, i.e., eight settings, all under the one envelope
of Section VI.
Workload and campaign. Each experiment block runs
every conﬁguration once, in an order that rotates between
blocks, with 65 READ polls and 64 select-before-operate
cycles each; we count the SELECT and the OPERATE of
a cycle as two transactions. The campaign ran 154 blocks
without failure, including pilot and feasibility blocks, and
its 128 main blocks produced 98,816 DNP3 transactions, 16
blocks per setting. The ﬁrst 8 blocks of each setting train the
attackers and the last 8 test them, so no tested transaction was
seen in training.
Attacker. We use the classiﬁer of Formby et al. [11], a
multinomial naive Bayes over 200-bin histograms of CLRT,
and apply it to operations rather than device types. Its histograms have a last bin that collects every value above a threshold 𝐻, which we set to 1.05 times the largest training CLRT.
Because the adversary reads function codes (Section III), it
groups the CLRT values of one operation into windows of
𝐾 = 8, 32 and 64 transactions and classiﬁes each window as
READ, SELECT or OPERATE. Following our threat model,
the attacker is trained on unobfuscated traﬃc captured before
NOMAD is deployed and is applied afterwards. To bound what
it can learn once NOMAD is in place, we also retrain the same
classiﬁer on the traﬃc of each conﬁguration. In both cases, the
classiﬁer is trained on the ﬁrst 8 blocks of a setting and tested

F. Limitations
Our endpoints are OpenDNP3 software processes behind
one switch, and the outstation answers after a ﬁxed delay;
physical relays answer with a wider timing spread, which
Strategy 1 should replace the same way but which we did
not measure on this build. The results are transaction-class
identiﬁcation by the classiﬁer of Formby et al., which uses
CLRT, not device identiﬁcation. Because the adversary reads
function codes, the operation of a window is already visible;
the classiﬁcation measures how much operation information
CLRT carries, which a device ﬁngerprint built on the same
histograms would use, not information the adversary otherwise
lacks. Response sizes and device-speciﬁc content such as
object groups, point counts and status bits remain readable
in plain text. With one outstation of nearly constant service
time, RO1’s independence from how fast a device serves a
request follows from the design (Section V), and we did not
test it across devices. A device that piggybacks its acknowledgment, like the ION7550, would follow the 𝑝𝑏 path, and its
master-visible timing would diﬀer from that of a device that
acknowledges separately. Our outstation sends no unsolicited
responses, so we did not measure PRT, and the OPERATE
hold was evaluated only on the acknowledgment and response
it shapes. Holding OPERATE delays the physical execution of
a command by 𝐽 , which an operator must weigh against the
timing requirements of protection functions. NOMAD leaves
requests native, and their sizes alone separate READ from
select-before-operate. The cause of the operation-dependent
oﬀset at 𝐷 = 10 ms is not established, and the guards of the
envelope are margins chosen for the experiments rather than
proven bounds.
VIII. R ELATED WORK
Device ﬁngerprinting. Devices can be identiﬁed by what
they emit and how they communicate, even without access
to the payload. Kohno et al. ﬁngerprint a remote host from
the skew of its clock in TCP timestamps [20], and GTID
identiﬁes a device and its type from the timing signature
of its packets [21]. Website ﬁngerprinting reaches the same
goal from packet sizes, directions and timing over encrypted
channels, with attackers based on random forests [22], deep
learning [5], and timing alone [23]. NOMAD considers the
same passive observer, and its objective is to remove the
timing and size features that such attackers rely on from the
master-facing link.
ICS and power-system ﬁngerprinting. Formby et al.
introduce ﬁngerprinting to control systems and deﬁne the
cross-layer response time and the physical operation time of
a DNP3 outstation [11], and their measurements of substation
traﬃc show how regular these timings are [24]. Later work
ﬁngerprints ICS devices from inter-layer response processing
time [25], from the scan cycle of PLCs [26], from time delays
in industrial dataﬂows [27], from device physics [28], and
from behavior under challenge and response [29], and Keliris
and Maniatakos identify Modbus ﬁeld devices from device-

speciﬁc protocol content [30], which NOMAD’s strategies
leave readable.
Such knowledge lets an adversary craft attacks that must
match the target, e.g., false data injection against state estimation [15] and attacks on process control [31]. Defenses against
reconnaissance in power grids have so far worked on content
and connectivity. DefRec virtualizes physical functions and
presents deceptive measurements [32], RAINCOAT randomizes data acquisition and injects decoy measurements [33], and
a testbed evaluates such approaches on grid infrastructure [34].
Compared to these defenses, NOMAD serves a diﬀerent
objective: it leaves the content of every control unit unchanged
and changes when and in which layout the outstation’s answer
reaches the master.
General traﬃc obfuscation. Traﬃc morphing transforms
one packet-size distribution into another [3], and Dyer et al.
show that per-packet padding and fragmentation still leave
exploitable features [4]. Tamaraw ﬁxes packet sizes and rates
at a bandwidth cost [6], WTF-PAD ﬁlls inter-packet gaps
adaptively [35], Walkie-Talkie molds bursts into supersequences [36], TraﬃcSliver splits traﬃc across paths [37], and
FRONT, Surakav and RegulaTor trade bandwidth and latency
in diﬀerent ways [38]–[40].
Closer to NOMAD’s setting, Handley et al. normalize
ambiguous traﬃc at a network element [41], Smart et al.
rewrite TCP/IP behavior to defeat stack ﬁngerprinting [42],
and Askarov et al. mitigate timing channels by releasing
outputs on a predicted schedule [43], the idea behind NOMAD’s deadline release. Shaping also protects smart-home
devices [44], and Pacer and NetShaper bound network sidechannel leakage in the cloud with shaped schedules [45], [46].
These defenses are eﬀective over encrypted channels with
cooperating end hosts that can add cover traﬃc. However,
in a plain-text ICS network, dummy packets and ﬁller bytes
are visible to an observer who parses the protocol, and the
outstation cannot be modiﬁed. NOMAD, on the other hand,
changes only the timing and the layout of packets the outstation
already sends.
Programmable data planes. P4 deﬁnes the programmable
parser and match/action model that NOMAD uses [12]. PIFO
provides programmable scheduling at line rate [47], SPPIFO approximates it with strict-priority queues [48], and
programmable calendar queues release packets by time on
Toﬁno, recirculating a marker packet whose arrival shows that
a queue has drained [49]; NOMAD’s blocker tokens play a
similar role for a single held packet.
On programmable switches, NetHide obfuscates the observable topology [50], Ditto shapes WAN traﬃc to a ﬁxed
pattern with a switch at each end [7], and Minos and Securitas
obfuscate encrypted traﬃc with padding, dummy packets
and fragmentation [8], [9]. In ICS networks, programmable
switches enhance protocol security [51] and correlate timed
events with deep packet inspection [52]. Compared to this
work, NOMAD runs on one switch at the outstation edge and
needs no de-obfuscation unit, because every packet it releases
remains a valid DNP3 packet for the unmodiﬁed master.

IX. CONCLUSION
In this paper, we presented NOMAD, an in-network traﬃc
obfuscation mechanism against ICS device ﬁngerprinting over
plain-text communication. NOMAD uses the syntax of control
units to apply a diﬀerent strategy to each type of operation on
one programmable switch: it replaces the CLRT of READ and
SELECT with a conﬁgured value, holds OPERATE commands
inside the switch, and splits the control units of every response
into one ﬁxed layout. On a Toﬁno-1 testbed with 98,816 DNP3
transactions, the CLRT at the master stayed within 10–16 𝜇s
of the conﬁgured value at the median, and all responses carried
61 bytes and remained conformant to IEEE 1815. The CLRT
classiﬁer of Formby et al., trained before deployment, fell to
chance in every setting, as it also did under a constant delay,
and the same classiﬁer retrained after deployment stayed near
its unobfuscated accuracy on our software outstation, whose
unobfuscated CLRT barely diﬀers between operations. All
transactions completed within 53 ms without retransmission.
In future work, we will evaluate NOMAD with physical relays
of several vendors, whose wider timing gives the classiﬁer
more to learn, and extend the strategies to requests.

A PPENDIX B
IMPLEMENTATION ON TOFINO-1
We implement the three modules of Section IV as three P416
programs for the Toﬁno Native Architecture, compiled with

p4c 9.13. The switch has two physical pipes. The classiﬁer
runs in the ingress of pipe 0 and hands the requests of timed
operations to the holder, which runs in the ingress of pipe 1;
the rewriter runs in the egress of pipe 0, through which every
packet toward an endpoint leaves. Each pipe has dedicated
recirculation ports, which carry the blocker tokens and the
held packets between passes. The holder reads the ingress
timestamp in units of 256 ns, and the rewriter updates the
TCP checksum incrementally. The classiﬁer and the holder
each use 11 match/action stages and the rewriter uses all 12
stages of its pipe, and on average the programs occupy 16.5%
of the SRAM and 31.3% of the TCAM of pipe 0.
