\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

We evaluate NOMAD against the three research objectives of Section~\ref{sec:overview}. For RO1, we measure whether the timing that the master observes equals the configured values; for RO2, we measure response sizes and protocol conformance; and for RO3, we measure the delay and the switch resources NOMAD needs. Because the objectives exist to defeat fingerprinting, we also measure what a transaction classifier learns from the obfuscated traffic.

\subsection{Testbed and Methodology}

\textbf{Testbed.} We run NOMAD on an Intel Tofino-1 switch with SDE~9.13.2. The DNP3 master and outstation are OpenDNP3~\cite{opendnp3} endpoints in two network namespaces of one server, connected through the same switch port, so every packet between them crosses the switch, and the outstation answers a request after a fixed 5~ms application delay. We capture every packet on the server's switch-facing interface with microsecond timestamps, once as the endpoints send it and once as the switch delivers it; the master-facing observation of Section~\ref{sec:overview} is the delivered stream.

\textbf{Configurations.} We compare four configurations on the same compiled image: \emph{No obfuscation}, \emph{Timing} (Strategies~1 and~2), \emph{Size} (Strategy~3) and \emph{Timing+Size} (all three). We vary the configured CLRT $D$ over 5, 10, 15 and 20~ms with $J$ = 2.4~ms, and the OPERATE hold $J$ over 5, 10, 15 and 20~ms with $D$ = 10~ms, i.e., eight settings, all under the one envelope of Section~\ref{sec:objectives}.

\textbf{Workload and campaign.} Each experiment block runs every configuration once, in an order that rotates between blocks, with 65 READ polls and 64 select-before-operate cycles each; we count the SELECT and the OPERATE of a cycle as two transactions. The campaign ran 154 blocks without failure, including pilot and feasibility blocks, and its 128 main blocks produced 98,816 DNP3 transactions, 16 blocks per setting. The first 8 blocks of each setting train the attackers and the last 8 test them, so no tested transaction was seen in training.

\textbf{Attackers.} Following Formby et al.~\cite{formby2016}, the adversary classifies each transaction as READ, SELECT or OPERATE from the master-facing link, with a Random-Forest classifier of 200 trees. A \emph{fixed} attacker is trained on unobfuscated traffic and applied unchanged after deployment, as in our threat model. An \emph{adaptive} attacker is retrained on each configuration and therefore knows the obfuscation. Each attacker uses four feature sets: CLRT; the request-to-acknowledgment time with CLRT; the response size; and all three together (\emph{joint}). We report balanced accuracy, for which chance among three operations is 1/3. The primary statistic is the reduction in balanced accuracy of the fixed joint attacker from No obfuscation to Timing+Size at $D$ = 10~ms and $J$ = 2.4~ms, with a 95\% interval from 10,000 paired bootstrap resamples of the 8 test blocks. Intervals for other settings are pointwise. % C8, C12, C15

\textbf{CLRT follows the configuration.} In Fig.~\ref{fig:clrt} (left), we show the CLRT the master measures in every configuration at $D$ = 10~ms. Without Strategy~1, CLRT stays at the 5.3~ms the outstation needs, and response-size normalization alone leaves it there. With Strategy~1, the distribution moves to the configured 10~ms and stays within 0.1~ms of it, in two modes about 40~$\mu$s apart. Fig.~\ref{fig:precision} quantifies the error over all eight settings: the median of $|\mathrm{CLRT} - D|$ is 10--16~$\mu$s for every operation, the 99th percentile is 58--86~$\mu$s, and 0.14--0.46\% of the transactions exceed 100~$\mu$s. The few exceptions form a tail near 1~ms, with a maximum of 1.93~ms. On an earlier build, we localized this tail to a host-observed delay downstream of the switch's egress parser, and we did not re-measure that attribution on this build. % C4, C16

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/nomad_eval/fig_precision_tail}
\caption{Complementary CDF of $|\mathrm{CLRT} - D|$ for the timed configurations over all eight settings, per operation; the dashed line marks 100~$\mu$s.}
\label{fig:precision}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{A small operation-dependent offset.} Fig.~\ref{fig:clrt} (right) breaks the error down by setting. At $D$ = 5, 15 and 20~ms, the three operations agree within 3~$\mu$s, at about 0, +7 and +11~$\mu$s. At $D$ = 10~ms, in all five settings of $J$, the medians of READ and SELECT sit 20--29~$\mu$s early while OPERATE stays within 5~$\mu$s of $D$; because the timed distribution has two modes (Fig.~\ref{fig:clrt}, left), the offset may reflect how often each operation falls in the earlier mode. We have not established the cause of this offset, which is far below the 5.3~ms CLRT of the outstation, but it is the one timing difference that remains, and Section~\ref{sec:eval:attack} shows that a retrained attacker uses it. % C4

\textbf{Replacement rather than shift.} A constant hold would keep the device's distribution and only move it. To show the difference, we shifted the unobfuscated CLRT at the primary setting by one constant onto the obfuscated median: the shifted OPERATE stays about 60~$\mu$s below READ and SELECT, while the measured Timing+Size distributions of the three operations lie within 25~$\mu$s of each other. The median variance of CLRT in held-out windows of 32 transactions is about three times lower with timing than without it. The native spread of our software outstation is already small, with a standard deviation of 0.06--0.11~ms, so the reduction is modest in absolute terms. % C5

\textbf{The OPERATE hold stays inside the switch.} Fig.~\ref{fig:operate} (left, a) shows that the hold measured before delivery follows the configured $J$ in every setting. Fig.~\ref{fig:operate} (left, b) shows that the master nevertheless sees the same request-to-response time of 41.1~ms for every $J$, because $d_a$ covers the hold (Section~\ref{sec:strategies}). Consequently, the time a device spends on a command no longer reaches the master-facing link. % C6

\subsection{RO2: Uniform and Conformant Responses}

Table~\ref{tab:delivery} summarizes every response of the campaign. Without Strategy~3, READ responses carry 49~bytes and SELECT and OPERATE responses 37~bytes; with it, every response carries 61~bytes, and requests keep their native 20 and 35~bytes. All 98,816 responses passed the IEEE~1815 conformance checker of our harness with intact transport and application sequence numbers, and no DNP3 CRC failed. In each size-normalized configuration, 16,384 select-before-operate responses travelled as three transport frames, and the master reassembled every one of them; every READ matched the outstation's values, and every SELECT authorized its OPERATE, without any packet lost, duplicated or reordered. % C7

\subsection{Resistance to Transaction Fingerprinting}
\label{sec:eval:attack}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/nomad_eval/fig_attackers}
\caption{Balanced accuracy of the fixed (top) and adaptive (bottom) Random-Forest attacker for four feature sets and all eight settings ($D$/$J$ in ms). The dashed line is chance.}
\label{fig:attackers}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{The fixed attacker.} In Fig.~\ref{fig:attackers}, we show both attackers in every setting. Trained on unobfuscated traffic, the fixed joint attacker identifies the operation with a balanced accuracy of 0.94--0.97, and under Timing+Size it falls to 0.333 in every setting. At the primary setting, the reduction is 0.617, with a 95\% interval of [0.593, 0.635]. Fig.~\ref{fig:confusion} shows why: at the primary setting, the fixed attacker labels every obfuscated transaction READ. % C8

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/nomad_eval/fig_confusion}
\caption{Row-normalized confusion matrices of the joint attacker at $D$ = 10~ms, $J$ = 2.4~ms.}
\label{fig:confusion}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{The adaptive attacker.} Part of the fixed attacker's drop comes from meeting unfamiliar traffic. With the joint or size features, the fixed attacker also falls to chance under Size alone in seven of the eight settings, where a retrained attacker recovers 0.58--0.65. We therefore treat the adaptive attacker as the conservative measure, and under Timing+Size the adaptive joint attacker reaches 0.36--0.46. Fig.~\ref{fig:operate} (right) shows the reduction along $D$ and $J$: 0.61--0.64 for the fixed joint attacker and 0.50--0.61 for the adaptive one. % C8, C12

\textbf{Where the remaining information sits.} To locate the residual, we retrained the adaptive attacker on groups of fields after the campaign; these analyses are post hoc. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:residual}(a), the request alone gives 0.667 in every setting. This is because NOMAD leaves requests native: a READ request carries 20~bytes and a SELECT or OPERATE request 35~bytes, which separates READ from select-before-operate but not SELECT from OPERATE. The defended response alone gives 0.35--0.47, and request and response together give 0.69--0.80, the same level as a diagnostic attacker that uses all observed metadata. Within the response, the confusion matrix in Fig.~\ref{fig:confusion} shows that READ and SELECT are indistinguishable and OPERATE is recognized in 51\% of the cases, which matches the 25~$\mu$s offset at $D$ = 10~ms. Fig.~\ref{fig:residual}(b) confirms it: READ against SELECT drops from perfect separation to 0.48--0.55 once the response size is normalized, and an attacker that predicts whether a READ followed a long or short gap stays at 0.46--0.57 under timing. % C9, C10

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/nomad_eval/fig_residual_sources}
\caption{Post hoc analysis with the adaptive attacker. (a) Three-way accuracy under Timing+Size by the fields used. (b) READ against SELECT (filled) and READ split by its preceding request gap (open), per configuration.}
\label{fig:residual}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Windows of transactions.} An attacker can also pool several transactions. A Naive-Bayes attacker on windows of $K$ = 8, 32 and 64 CLRT values of the same operation, which requires knowing the operation of each transaction, stays at chance against the fixed model under Timing+Size. Its adaptive accuracy varies widely between settings, because only 24 test windows per setting remain at $K$ = 64, and a window of 64 obfuscated transactions spans about 10~s of traffic. % C11

\subsection{RO3: Overhead}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.49\textwidth}\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/nomad_eval/fig_latency}\end{minipage}\hfill

\caption{Left: request-to-response time at the master per setting ($D$/$J$ in ms), median (marker) to maximum (line), with the endpoint timers. Right: response availability after the request at the outstation side, against the envelope of Section~\ref{sec:objectives}.}
\label{fig:latency}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Delay.} In Fig.~\ref{fig:latency} (left), we show the request-to-response time at the master. Without timing, a transaction completes in 5.4~ms at the median, and with timing it completes at $d_a + D$, i.e., 36.1--51.1~ms at the median, while the slowest of all 49,408 timed transactions took 52.3~ms. Every transaction completed below the 201~ms TCP retransmission timeout and the 500~ms response timeout of the master, and no request was retransmitted (Table~\ref{tab:delivery}). % C14

\textbf{Envelope coverage.} Fig.~\ref{fig:latency} (right) compares the time each response became available at the outstation side with the envelope. Unobfuscated responses were available within 8.0~ms and held OPERATE responses within 26.45~ms, and no response of any configuration arrived after $d_a$ = 31~ms. Consequently, every timed acknowledgment committed at its deadline, and no fallback release occurred (Table~\ref{tab:delivery}); about one blocker token for every two timed transactions outlived its packet and was retired, and delivery was unaffected. The largest availability exceeded the pilot estimate $L$ = 5.598~ms, which the guard $G$ absorbed. % C13

\textbf{Switch resources.} NOMAD fits one Tofino-1 switch, and its three programs leave most of the memory of each pipe free (Table~\ref{tab:resources}). % C3

\subsection{Limitations}

Our endpoints are OpenDNP3 software processes behind one switch, and the outstation answers after a fixed delay; physical relays answer with a wider timing spread, which Strategy~1 should replace the same way but which we did not measure on this build. The results are transaction-class identification for the evaluated attackers, not device identification, and the attackers use metadata, i.e., sizes, packet counts and timing; device-specific content such as object groups, point counts and status bits remains readable in plain text. With one outstation of nearly constant service time, RO1's independence from how fast a device serves a request follows from the design (Section~\ref{sec:strategies}), and we did not test it across devices. A device that piggybacks its acknowledgment, like the ION7550, would follow the $p_b$ path, and its master-visible timing would differ from that of a device that acknowledges separately. Holding OPERATE delays the physical execution of a command by $J$, which an operator must weigh against the timing requirements of protection functions. NOMAD leaves requests native, and the request alone separates READ from select-before-operate. The cause of the operation-dependent offset at $D$ = 10~ms is not established, and the guards of the envelope are margins chosen for the experiments rather than proven bounds.
