\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Device Fingerprinting.} Devices can be identified from what they emit, without access to the
payload. Kohno et al.\ fingerprint a remote host from the skew of its clock in TCP
timestamps~\cite{kohno2005}, and GTID identifies a device and its type from the
timing signature of its packets~\cite{gtid2015}. These works define
the threat we address, and each of them reads a different observable. Compared to them, we target the
interval that the cross-layer response-time method of Formby et al.\ classifies by, and we replace
that interval on the master-facing link.

\textbf{ICS and Power-System Fingerprinting.} Formby et al.\ brought fingerprinting to control
systems with the cross-layer response time and the physical operation time of DNP3
outstations~\cite{formby2016}, and Gu et al.\ added device physics as a
feature~\cite{gu2018}. Pospisil et al.\ identify PLC models
passively from the time delay in their
dataflow~\cite{pospisil2023}, which shows that execution time still
identifies industrial devices under other protocols. The reconnaissance these techniques serve
enables false data injection and process-control
attacks~\cite{liufdia2009, cardenas2011}. Consequently, the
interval our framework replaces is the observable this line of work relies on.

\textbf{Anti-Reconnaissance in Power Grids.} The defenses closest to ours change what the traffic says
and which nodes it reaches. DefRec virtualizes physical functions and crafts decoy data for virtual
nodes~\cite{defrec2020}, RAINCOAT randomizes data acquisition and injects
decoy measurements~\cite{raincoat2019}, and a companion testbed evaluates such
approaches on grid infrastructure~\cite{lintestbed2020}. These approaches are
effective against reconnaissance of network and physical configurations. However, they build their
protection above the packets an outstation sends, so the execution time of a real device still
travels with its answer. Our framework, on the other hand, works below the semantics they reshape
and changes when the wire carries that answer, which makes the two complementary.

\textbf{General Traffic Obfuscation.} Traffic morphing transforms the packet-size distribution of one
class into another~\cite{wright2009}, while Dyer et al.\ show that per-packet
padding and fragmentation still leave exploitable features~\cite{dyer2012}. Tamaraw
fixes packet size and rate at a bandwidth cost~\cite{tamaraw2014}, and
WTF-PAD pads inter-packet gaps adaptively~\cite{juarez2016}. These
defenses are effective against the flow-level features they target, and the attacks that follow them
have become correspondingly
strong~\cite{sirinam2018, panchenko2016}.
The same size-and-timing channel identifies smart-home devices under encryption, where shaping is the
countermeasure~\cite{apthorpe2019}, and Pacer and NetShaper shape the traffic of a
tenant to a schedule that bounds side-channel leakage in the
cloud~\cite{pacer2022, netshaper2024}. Pacer and
NetShaper each shape from a host or a hypervisor that can add cover traffic, and the smart-home
setting assumes an encrypted payload. Compared to these settings, ours carries a plaintext protocol
whose leaking feature is one interval inside a single request-response exchange, and no endpoint can
be modified to help. Consequently, our framework adds no byte and runs entirely in the network.

\textbf{Programmable Data-Plane Traffic Transformation.} P4 defines the programmable parser and
match-action model we use~\cite{p4ccr2014}. PIFO defines
programmable scheduling at line rate~\cite{pifo2016}, and SP-PIFO
approximates it with the strict-priority queues of today's
switches~\cite{sppifo2020}, which is the primitive our blocker queues use.
Ditto pads packets and injects chaff to a fixed pattern at line rate,
and it carries up to 70~Gbps of production traffic on a 100~Gbps
link~\cite{ditto2022}. Minos morphs and schedules traffic on a programmable switch at
low cost~\cite{minos2025}, and Securitas combines fragmentation and insertion
across several data planes~\cite{securitas2026}. NetHide obfuscates topology in
the data plane against link-flooding reconnaissance~\cite{nethide2018}. Closest
to our setting, Hu et al.\ use programmable switches to enhance the security of industrial
protocols~\cite{hulin2023}. These systems reshape which packets a link carries
and how large they are. Our framework, on the other hand, addresses one interval inside a
transaction, and it holds packets rather than adding or reshaping them.

\textbf{DNP3 Timing and Security.} East et al.\ catalogue attacks on DNP3 and the absence of
encryption that makes its semantics visible~\cite{east2009}, and
specification-based and state-based intrusion detection has been adapted to
DNP3~\cite{linbro2013, fovino2010}. These defenses inspect what a
packet says, and none of them changes the timing an adversary sees. On the control path, Formby et
al.\ read the physical operation time from an event timestamp inside the application
payload~\cite{formby2016}, which is a different channel from the packet timing our
framework replaces.

\textbf{Protecting the Framework Itself.} Like any in-network defense, the framework adds a switch to
what an operator must protect, and an adversary that compromises the switch sees every hold. However,
the framework makes light use of the switch. One pipeline program holds the packets of three
function codes and forwards all others unchanged, no controller sits in the forwarding path, and the
control plane reads back every policy it installs. Checking this program and its policy is a smaller
task than hardening the legacy outstations behind it, which often cannot be patched.
