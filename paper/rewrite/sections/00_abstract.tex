\begin{abstract}
Device fingerprinting is a first step of cyber reconnaissance against industrial control
systems. In a power grid, a passive observer on the control network can tell the model of a
DNP3 outstation and the type of operation it performs from the timing of its responses, and
this timing stays visible when the payload is encrypted. Traffic-obfuscation defenses built
for the Internet pad, split and delay packets on the end host, which legacy control traffic
does not tolerate: DNP3 validates every block of its payload, and protective relays cannot be
changed to shape their own traffic. In this paper, we present a timing-obfuscation framework
that runs in the data plane of one programmable switch between the master and the outstation.
The framework classifies each DNP3 transaction, holds the packets that carry the outstation's
timing, and releases them at configurable offsets, so the timing the observer measures is a
policy value rather than the device's own. We apply it to the two timing features of Formby et
al.\ as case studies: the cross-layer response time (CLRT) of READ and of the SELECT phase of
select-before-operate control, and the master-visible timing of an OPERATE. We implement the
framework as one P4 program on an Intel Tofino-1 and evaluate it against a physical SEL-751A
relay. With the timing mechanism off, the relay's CLRT spreads over roughly 1 to 18~ms and
differs between READ and SELECT; with it on, both settle at a median of 4.001~ms with a standard
deviation of 0.02~ms. The master-visible OPERATE timing stays at about 4.00~ms across three
configured internal holds. The mutual information between transaction class and CLRT falls from 0.42~bits to
below 0.003~bits, inside its permutation null, and a READ-versus-SELECT classifier falls from
0.59 balanced accuracy to chance. The results show the suppression of a timing channel on one
relay in one capture session, not the concealment of the function codes that remain visible,
and not indistinguishability across devices.
\end{abstract}
