\begin{abstract}
An adversary watching a power-grid control network learns what an outstation is doing from how
long it takes to answer: the interval between the device's transport acknowledgment and its
application response reflects the work the device performed, and Formby et al.\ showed that its
distribution identifies device types. The relay cannot be modified, and existing traffic
obfuscation reshapes flow-level sizes and inter-packet gaps rather than one interval inside a
single transaction.

We place the defense in the network. A programmable switch in front of the outstation holds the
acknowledgment and the response and releases each at a deadline computed from the moment the
outstation acknowledged the request. Because both deadlines share that instant, the device's
processing time cancels from the gap between the two releases, and the interval an observer
measures becomes a value the operator sets. The switch forwards the packets it received, so both
endpoints run unmodified and no DNP3 byte changes.

We implement the framework on an Intel Tofino-1 and evaluate it against a physical SEL-751A
protective relay over 63{,}360 exchanges. The released interval settles on the configured 4~ms, and its
standard deviation falls from 2.609 to 0.628~ms, and the operator can place it anywhere from 1 to
22~ms at a fixed budget without moving the response time the master sees. The mutual information
between the interval and the transaction class falls into its permutation null, and a classifier
trained on Timing OFF traffic drops from 0.745 balanced accuracy to three-class chance. An adversary
that retrains and pools twenty exchanges into a signature stays at chance on the replaced interval,
where Timing OFF traffic lets it reach 0.988.
\end{abstract}
