\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

How long an outstation takes to answer has become a signature an observer can profile, and the
relay leaking it cannot be modified. Consequently this paper presents a timing-obfuscation
framework that moves the problem into the network, placing a switch in front of the outstation
that holds the packets carrying the device's timing and releases each at a deadline computed when
the transaction begins. The read
path, which is READ and the SELECT phase of select-before-operate, times that deadline from the
outstation's acknowledgment; OPERATE times it from the request. Consequently, the interval an observer
sees is the operator's value rather than the device's.

Evaluating this on one physical relay shows the released interval concentrating on the configured
value, its interquartile range falling from 2.78 to 0.006~ms and its standard deviation from 2.61
to 0.63~ms, while a classifier trained on that interval before deployment drops from 0.745
balanced accuracy to three-class chance. The framework protects the exchanges its budget can schedule and
forwards the rest untouched.

Three bounds limit the result. An adversary who retrains recovers to 0.782 by adding the
request-to-acknowledgment interval, which the mechanism does not target. The evidence comes from
one relay and one campaign, and it separates transaction classes, not device models. Since the switch's own release instants were never captured, the residual around the configured
value measures a difference between two releases, which we cannot place inside the switch. An
instrumented build measures an internal blocker-termination interval of about 1.7~$\mu$s,
characterizing the mechanism without decomposing that residual. Evaluating the
framework on outstations from several vendors, and capturing the release instants on both switch
links, is what would attribute it.
