\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

How long an outstation takes to answer is a signature an observer can profile, and Formby
et al.~\cite{formbyWhosControlYour2016} classify devices by it. We remove that signature in the
network. A switch in front of the unmodified outstation holds the packets that carry the device's
timing and releases each at a deadline computed when the transaction begins, so the interval an
observer sees is the operator's value rather than the device's.

On a physical SEL-751A the released interval settles on the configured value, its interquartile
range falling from 2.778 to 0.006~ms and its standard deviation from 2.609 to 0.628~ms, and the mutual
information it carries about the transaction class falls into its permutation null. A classifier
trained on Timing OFF traffic drops from 0.745 balanced accuracy to three-class chance. An adversary
that retrains on the replaced interval and pools twenty exchanges into a signature, as Formby et
al.\ do, stays at chance, where the same adversary on Timing OFF traffic reaches 0.988. What an
adversary can still learn comes from the request-to-acknowledgment interval, whose structure comes from the
switch's own release logic rather than from the device. The operator chooses the released interval anywhere
from 1 to 22~ms at a fixed budget, and the switch adds no byte or frame to do it.
