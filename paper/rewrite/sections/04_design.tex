\section{Design}
\label{sec:design}

In this section, we first present an overview of the framework. Then we describe how it replaces
the CLRT of a read transaction (Section~\ref{sec:design:read}) and of a control transaction
(Section~\ref{sec:design:control}), each followed by its security argument. Last, we argue why its
overhead is negligible and bounded (Section~\ref{sec:design:overhead}).

\subsection{Design Overview}
\label{sec:design:overview}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\IfFileExists{figures/fig_design.pdf}{\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig_design}}{\figplaceholder{One P4 pipeline read left to right: everything on the left arrives at the switch and everything on the right leaves it. The request arms both release deadlines; the read lane holds the acknowledgment and the response, each behind its own queue of blocker packets; the control lane holds a command the same way; a strict-priority scheduler drains each blocker queue first and the blocker loopback returns into it.}}
\caption{The framework on one switch pipeline, read left to right. A request arms both release
  deadlines from its own arrival, so neither instant carries anything the outstation did. The
  scheduler drains each blocker queue before the queue it guards, which is what holds a packet until
  its deadline. The figure does not show the exchanges the switch forwards unchanged.}
\label{fig:design}
\end{figure*}

In Figure~\ref{fig:design}, we present the design of the framework. We place a programmable switch
between the master and the outstation, and the switch classifies every DNP3 exchange by its function
code. Based on that classification, it holds the packets that carry the timing of the outstation and
releases each of them at a deadline, such that the interval the adversary measures becomes the value
the operator configures. We handle two kinds of transactions in two lanes.

\textbf{Holding a Packet.} A switch pipeline carries no timer a program can wait on, so the
framework holds a packet with the queues instead. Every packet it delays waits in a queue of its
own, beneath a second queue of higher priority that carries \emph{blocker packets}, which the
switch generates itself. While blocker packets keep arriving, the scheduler serves them and never
serves the packet beneath them. The hold ends when the blocker queue runs dry at the deadline, and
the held packet leaves on the next service. Blocker packets circulate on a loopback port inside the
switch, so they reach neither the master nor the outstation and consume no bandwidth on either
link. Section~\ref{sec:impl} describes how the program generates them and how it decides when they
stop.

\textbf{Read Lane.} For a READ poll and for the SELECT phase of a control command, the framework
holds the transport acknowledgment and the application response of the outstation on their way to
the master. It computes both release deadlines when the acknowledgment arrives
(Section~\ref{sec:design:read}).

\textbf{Control Lane.} For the OPERATE phase, the framework also holds the command itself on its way
to the relay. Because the acknowledgment then no longer marks the start of the work, the framework
computes the deadlines when the OPERATE request arrives (Section~\ref{sec:design:control}).

Both lanes forward the packets they receive at their original size and generate none on either
link. The master and the outstation exchange exactly the packets they would exchange
without the framework, and only the instants at which those packets leave the switch differ.

\subsection{Replacing the CLRT of a Read Transaction}
\label{sec:design:read}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{figures/model/fig_m01_release_timeline}
  \caption{Release timeline of one READ transaction, drawn schematically. (a) The response arrives in
  time. (b) It arrives late and leaves when its queue next serves it.}
  \label{fig:timeline}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Timing Model.} In Figure~\ref{fig:timeline}, we show the timeline of one READ transaction.
The master sends a request, and the outstation answers it twice: its transport layer first
acknowledges the request, and its application later returns the response. We write $t_A$ and $t_R$
for the instants the acknowledgment and the response reach the switch, and $e_A$ and $e_R$ for the
instants the switch forwards them. Without the framework, the adversary measures
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:clrt-original}
\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{original}} = t_R - t_A ,
\end{equation}
which is dominated by the execution time of the outstation (Section~\ref{sec:bg}). We write
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:holds}
D_A = e_A - t_A
\end{equation}
for the \emph{acknowledgment hold} and refer to $e_R - t_R$ as the \emph{response hold}. The interval
the adversary measures then becomes
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:clrt-new}
\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}} = e_R - e_A
  = \mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{original}} + (e_R - t_R) - D_A .
\end{equation}

\textbf{Constant Holds.} Equation~(\ref{eq:clrt-new}) shows why holding both packets for constant
times cannot remove the fingerprint. If both holds are constant, the adversary measures the original
interval shifted by their difference. The distribution moves along the time axis and keeps its
shape, e.g., a spread of 2.6~ms on our relay, so an adversary who has profiled the device still
recognizes it. To remove the fingerprint, the response hold must absorb the variation of
$\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{original}}$.

\textbf{Dual-Deadline Schedule.} To make the response hold absorb that variation, we compute both
release deadlines from the same instant $t_A$. When the acknowledgment arrives, the framework sets
the scheduled instants
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:schedule}
\hat{e}_A = t_A + D_A , \qquad \hat{e}_R = t_A + D_A + \mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}} ,
\end{equation}
where the configured $\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$ is the interval the operator wants the adversary
to measure. Because neither deadline depends on $t_R$, the term $\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{original}}$
cancels in~(\ref{eq:clrt-new}), and the response hold becomes
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:coupling}
e_R - t_R = D_A + \mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}} - \mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{original}} .
\end{equation}
The response hold follows the device on every transaction. It is long when the outstation
answers quickly and short when the outstation answers slowly.

\textbf{Availability.} The switch can delay a packet but cannot release one it has not received. We
call
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:budget}
D = D_A + \mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}
\end{equation}
the \emph{release budget}, and the schedule requires
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:availability}
t_R \le t_A + D .
\end{equation}
A response that violates~(\ref{eq:availability}) arrives after its deadline. The framework marks it
late and forwards it as soon as its queue serves it, so the exchange completes with its own timing.
The operator chooses $D$ such that late responses are rare
(Section~\ref{sec:design:overhead}).

\textbf{Security Argument.} Each held packet leaves the switch slightly after its deadline, by a
\emph{release tail} $\varepsilon$ that the queues of the switch introduce
(Section~\ref{sec:impl}). Writing $e_A = \hat{e}_A + \varepsilon_A$ and
$e_R = \hat{e}_R + \varepsilon_R$, the schedule~(\ref{eq:schedule}) gives, for every response that
satisfies~(\ref{eq:availability}),
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:released}
e_R - e_A = \mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}} + \varepsilon_R - \varepsilon_A .
\end{equation}
No term on the right-hand side depends on $t_R$. Consequently, the execution time of the outstation
does not reach the interval the adversary measures. The operator sets the configured value, and the
release tails belong to the switch rather than to the device. Nothing in this argument refers to which
operation the outstation performed or which device produced the response. A different operation and
a different device type are both a different distribution of
$\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{original}}$, and both cancel in the same way. Therefore, a classifier that
separates device types or operation types by the CLRT, such as the one of Formby et
al.~\cite{formbyWhosControlYour2016}, receives the same input distribution for every class, which
makes it challenging, if not impossible, to perform better than chance. Pooling does not help the
adversary either, since pooling $k$ draws from one distribution yields one distribution for every
class, whatever $k$ is. The device can still reach the interval in only two ways, through release
tails that depend on the length of the hold and through responses that
violate~(\ref{eq:availability}). We evaluate both in Section~\ref{sec:eval}.

\textbf{Is It Possible to Recover the Execution Time (Adaptive Adversary)?} An adaptive adversary
knows the framework and the configured $\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$, but this knowledge reveals
only a value the operator chose. The adversary can still measure the intervals the framework does
not replace. The request-to-acknowledgment interval carries the acknowledgment latency of the
outstation and the hold $D_A + \varepsilon_A$. The acknowledgment latency reflects the transport
stack of the outstation rather than its application, e.g., our relay acknowledges READ, SELECT and
OPERATE requests within 0.003~ms of one another (Section~\ref{sec:bg}). Consequently, any class
structure this interval shows under the framework comes from the release logic of the switch, e.g.,
from the two lanes timing their deadlines from different events, rather than from the execution
time of the device. We quantify this residual in Section~\ref{sec:eval:ro2}.

\subsection{Replacing the CLRT of a Control Transaction}
\label{sec:design:control}

A select-before-operate control command differs from a poll in one way. The framework also holds
the OPERATE request before it reaches the relay, which separates the instant the master issues the
command from the instant the relay acts on it. The acknowledgment of the outstation then cannot be
the reference instant, because it exists only after the command is released, and a
deadline measured from it would carry the command hold into the interval the adversary measures. To
remove this dependence, we time the control lane from the request. When the OPERATE arrives at
$T_0$, the framework releases it toward the relay after a hold $J$ drawn from a configured set, and
it schedules the acknowledgment and the response toward the master at
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:control}
\hat{e}_{\mathrm{ack}} = T_0 + D_A , \qquad
\hat{e}_{\mathrm{or}} = \hat{e}_{\mathrm{ack}} + \mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}} .
\end{equation}
The two offsets are those of the read lane, and one set of deadline registers serves both lanes
(Section~\ref{sec:impl}).

\textbf{Security Argument.} The adversary measures
$O = e_{\mathrm{or}} - e_{\mathrm{ack}} = \mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{or}} -
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ack}}$, in which neither the execution time of the relay nor the command hold
$J$ appears. As such, the framework can vary the instant the relay acts on a command while the
interval the adversary measures stays fixed, and the adversary cannot subtract a known offset to
recover either quantity. As in the read lane, the schedule requires the response of the relay to
reach the switch before $\hat{e}_{\mathrm{or}}$, i.e., within $D$ of the request, with the command hold
$J$ included.

\subsection{Overhead Argument}
\label{sec:design:overhead}

Obfuscation is only useful if an operator can afford it. We now argue why the framework introduces
negligible overhead (RO3) in any deployment.

\textbf{Bandwidth Overhead.} As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:design}, the framework forwards the
packets it receives at their original size.
The blocker packets that sustain a hold recirculate on internal loopback ports and never reach a
cable (Section~\ref{sec:impl}). Consequently, the framework adds no frame and no byte to either
link. Obfuscation that pads packets or injects dummy ones pays a bandwidth cost that this design
does not have~\cite{meierDittoWANTraffic2022, dyerPeekaBooStillSee2012}.

\textbf{Latency Bound.} We delay two packets per transaction. The acknowledgment leaves
$D_A + \varepsilon_A$ after it arrives. A response that satisfies~(\ref{eq:availability}) leaves at
$t_A + D + \varepsilon_R$, so by~(\ref{eq:coupling}) its added delay,
$D - \mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{original}} + \varepsilon_R$, never exceeds $D + \varepsilon_R$ and
shrinks as the outstation answers more slowly. A response that violates~(\ref{eq:availability})
leaves as soon as its queue serves it. The operator therefore bounds every added delay by choosing
the release budget $D$, and the release tail is the only term the operator does not set.

\textbf{Protocol Timers.} Three timers of the two endpoints can observe these delays. The TCP stack of the master
retransmits a request whose acknowledgment does not arrive within its retransmission timeout
$\tau_m$. The TCP stack of the outstation retransmits a response the master does not acknowledge
within its own timeout $\tau_o$. The relay cancels a selected control point whose OPERATE does not
follow within the select-to-operate timeout $\tau_s$, 1.0~s by default on the
SEL-751A~\cite{selSEL751AInstructionManual}. The acknowledgment hold delays the first, the response
hold delays the second, and the hold on the SELECT response delays the third. Consequently, the
framework triggers none of them as long as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:timers}
D + \varepsilon < \min(\tau_m, \tau_o, \tau_s) .
\end{equation}
Condition~(\ref{eq:timers}) holds for any deployment whose outstation answers well within its
timers, because the budget only needs to cover the response time of the outstation. RFC~6298
recommends a lower bound of one second on the retransmission
timeout~\cite{paxsonComputingTCPsRetransmission2011}. On our testbed, the master repeats an
unacknowledged request after 201~ms and the relay repeats an unacknowledged response after about
3~s, while a 24~ms budget covers 99.9\% of the responses of the relay
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:perf}).

\textbf{Security-Performance Trade-off Argument.} The release budget $D$ sets both sides of a
trade-off. A larger budget places more of the responses of the outstation on the schedule, so fewer late
responses carry their own timing, and it adds more delay to every transaction.
Within a fixed budget, the split between $D_A$ and the configured $\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$
costs nothing, and the operator can place the released interval anywhere below $D$ without changing
the delay. We set $\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$ to a constant, because a constant removes the
variation of the device entirely and an operator can state and check it. The schedule can carry any
other value, e.g., one drawn from a distribution, without change.
