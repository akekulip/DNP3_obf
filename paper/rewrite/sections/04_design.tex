\section{Design}
\label{sec:design}

In this section, we first present an overview of the framework. Then we describe how it replaces
the CLRT of a read transaction (Section~\ref{sec:design:read}) and of a control transaction
(Section~\ref{sec:design:control}), each followed by its security argument. Last, we argue why its
overhead is negligible and bounded (Section~\ref{sec:design:overhead}).

\subsection{Design Overview}
\label{sec:design:overview}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig_design}
\caption{Packet flow and its mapping to the P4 pipeline. Ingress classifies packets, arms
  request-anchored deadlines and selects queues; the traffic manager provides the wait.
  ACK and response queues share one strict-priority scheduler, while OPERATE requests use
  a separate scheduler. Internal loopback bypasses egress. Returning blockers are re-enqueued
  or dropped according to expiry; after their queue drains, the released packet returns to
  ingress and follows the normal output path.
  Scheduled deadlines do not include the residual release tail.}
\label{fig:design}
\end{figure*}

In Figure~\ref{fig:design}, we present the design of the framework. We place a programmable switch
between the master and the outstation, and the switch classifies every DNP3 exchange by its function
code. Based on that classification, it holds the packets that carry the timing of the outstation and
releases each of them at a deadline, such that the interval the adversary measures becomes the value
the operator configures. We handle two kinds of transactions in two lanes.

\textbf{Holding a Packet.} A switch pipeline carries no timer a program can wait on, so the
framework holds a packet with the queues instead. Every packet it delays waits in a queue of its
own, beneath a second queue of higher priority that carries \emph{blocker packets}, which the
switch generates itself. While blocker packets keep arriving, the scheduler serves them and never
serves the packet beneath them. The hold ends when the blocker queue runs dry at the deadline, and
the held packet leaves on the next service. Blocker packets circulate on a loopback port inside the
switch, so they reach neither the master nor the outstation and consume no bandwidth on either
link. Section~\ref{sec:impl} describes how the program generates them and how it decides when they
stop.

\textbf{Read Lane.} For a READ poll and for the SELECT phase of a control command, the framework
holds the transport acknowledgment and the application response of the outstation on their way to
the master. It computes both release deadlines when the request arrives
(Section~\ref{sec:design:read}).

\textbf{Control Lane.} For the OPERATE phase, the framework also holds the command itself on its way
to the relay, and it computes the deadlines of the acknowledgment and the response from the arrival
of the OPERATE request with the offsets of the read lane (Section~\ref{sec:design:control}).

Both lanes forward the packets they receive at their original size and generate none on either
link. The master and the outstation exchange exactly the packets they would exchange
without the framework, and only the instants at which those packets leave the switch differ.

\subsection{Replacing the CLRT of a Read Transaction}
\label{sec:design:read}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{figures/model/fig_m01_release_timeline}
  \caption{Release timeline of one READ transaction. The five instants are all at the switch: the
  request arrives at $t_0$, the acknowledgment and the response of the outstation at $t_A$ and
  $t_R$, and each of them is released at $e_A$ and $e_R$. Both deadlines are armed from $t_0$,
  so neither depends on what the outstation did. (a) The response arrives in time. (b) It
  arrives late and leaves when its queue next serves it.}
  \label{fig:timeline}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Timing Model.} In Figure~\ref{fig:timeline}, we show the timeline of one READ transaction.
The request reaches the switch at $t_0$, and the outstation answers it twice: its transport layer
first acknowledges the request, and its application later returns the response. We write $t_A$ and
$t_R$ for the instants the acknowledgment and the response reach the switch, and $e_A$ and $e_R$ for
the instants the switch forwards them. Without the framework, the adversary measures
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:clrt-original}
\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{original}} = t_R - t_A ,
\end{equation}
which is dominated by the execution time of the outstation (Section~\ref{sec:bg}). With the
framework, the adversary measures
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:clrt-new}
\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}} = e_R - e_A
  = \mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{original}} + (e_R - t_R) - (e_A - t_A) ,
\end{equation}
where $e_A - t_A$ and $e_R - t_R$ are the \emph{acknowledgment hold} and the \emph{response hold},
the times the two packets spend in the switch.

\textbf{Constant Holds.} Equation~(\ref{eq:clrt-new}) shows why holding both packets for constant
times cannot remove the fingerprint. If both holds are constant, the adversary measures the original
interval shifted by their difference. The distribution moves along the time axis and keeps its
shape, e.g., a standard deviation of 2.889~ms for READ on our relay, so an adversary who has
profiled the device still recognizes it. To remove the fingerprint, the holds must absorb the
variation of the outstation.

\textbf{Dual-Deadline Schedule.} To make the holds absorb that variation, we compute both release
deadlines from the arrival of the request. When the request arrives at $t_0$, the framework sets the
scheduled instants
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:schedule}
\hat{e}_A = t_0 + D_A , \qquad \hat{e}_R = t_0 + D_A + \mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}} ,
\end{equation}
where $D_A$ is the \emph{acknowledgment deadline} and the configured
$\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$ is the interval the operator wants the adversary to measure. Neither
deadline depends on $t_A$ or $t_R$, so the two holds become
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:coupling}
e_A - t_A = D_A - (t_A - t_0) , \qquad
e_R - t_R = D_A + \mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}} - (t_R - t_0) .
\end{equation}
Both holds follow the device on every transaction. Each is long when the outstation answers quickly
and short when it answers slowly.

We anchor both deadlines at the request rather than at the acknowledgment for two reasons. First,
the acknowledgment is sent by the outstation, so a deadline measured from it would carry the
acknowledgment latency of the outstation into the request-to-acknowledgment interval, which the
adversary also measures. Second, the control lane must time its deadlines from the request
(Section~\ref{sec:design:control}). With one reference for both lanes, the lanes differ only in the
command they hold, and the adversary cannot use the reference itself to tell them apart.

\textbf{Availability.} The switch can delay a packet but cannot release one it has not received. We
call
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:budget}
D = D_A + \mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}
\end{equation}
the \emph{release budget}, and the schedule requires
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:availability}
t_R \le t_0 + D ,
\end{equation}
i.e., the budget must cover the whole time the outstation takes to answer a request. A response
that violates~(\ref{eq:availability}) arrives after its deadline. The framework marks it late and
forwards it as soon as its queue serves it, so the exchange completes with its own timing. The
operator chooses $D$ such that late responses are rare (Section~\ref{sec:design:overhead}).

\textbf{Security Argument.} Each held packet leaves the switch slightly after its deadline, by a
\emph{release tail} $\varepsilon$ that the queues of the switch introduce
(Section~\ref{sec:impl}). Writing $e_A = \hat{e}_A + \varepsilon_A$ and
$e_R = \hat{e}_R + \varepsilon_R$, the schedule~(\ref{eq:schedule}) gives, for every response that
satisfies~(\ref{eq:availability}),
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:released}
e_A - t_0 = D_A + \varepsilon_A , \qquad
e_R - e_A = \mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}} + \varepsilon_R - \varepsilon_A .
\end{equation}
No term on the right-hand side depends on $t_A$ or $t_R$. Consequently, the execution time of the
outstation reaches neither the request-to-acknowledgment interval nor the CLRT the adversary
measures. The operator sets the configured values, and the release tails belong to the switch: a
tail is the time a blocker queue takes to drain after its deadline, which depends on the state of
the queue when the request arrives and not on the device. Nothing in this argument refers to which
operation the outstation performed or which device produced the response. A different operation and
a different device type are both a different distribution of $t_A - t_0$ and $t_R - t_0$, and both
are absorbed in the same way by~(\ref{eq:coupling}). Therefore, a classifier that separates device
types or operation types by these intervals, such as the one of Formby et
al.~\cite{formby2016}, receives intervals set by the switch rather than by the
device. Pooling many exchanges cannot recover what the intervals do not contain. The device can
still reach the intervals in only one way, through responses that
violate~(\ref{eq:availability}), and we evaluate how often this happens in
Section~\ref{sec:eval}.

\textbf{Is It Possible to Recover the Execution Time (Adaptive Adversary)?} An adaptive adversary
knows the framework and the configured values, but this knowledge reveals only values the operator
chose. Retraining on obfuscated traffic gives it the intervals of~(\ref{eq:released}), which contain
the configured offsets and the release tails. A release tail depends on when a request reaches the
blocker queue, and the master decides when each request is sent, e.g., it sends an OPERATE
immediately after the response to its SELECT. Consequently, an adaptive adversary can learn from the
tails when requests reach the switch, which it can already read from the request timestamps, but
not how long the outstation takes to execute them. We quantify what it learns in
Section~\ref{sec:eval:ro2}.

\subsection{Replacing the CLRT of a Control Transaction}
\label{sec:design:control}

A select-before-operate control command differs from a poll in one way. The framework also holds
the OPERATE request before it reaches the relay, which separates the instant the master issues the
command from the instant the relay acts on it. When the OPERATE arrives at $T_0$, the framework
releases it toward the relay after a command hold $J$ drawn from a configured set, and it schedules
the acknowledgment and the response toward the master at
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:control}
\hat{e}_{\mathrm{ack}} = T_0 + D_A , \qquad
\hat{e}_{\mathrm{or}} = T_0 + D_A + \mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}} .
\end{equation}
The acknowledgment of the relay exists only after the command is released, so a deadline measured
from it would carry the command hold into the interval the adversary measures; the request is the
only reference that precedes both. The reference and the offsets are those of the read lane, and one
set of deadline registers serves both lanes (Section~\ref{sec:impl}).

\textbf{Security Argument.} The adversary measures
$O = e_{\mathrm{or}} - e_{\mathrm{ack}} = \mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{or}} -
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ack}}$, in which neither the execution time of the relay nor the command hold
$J$ appears. As such, the framework can vary the instant the relay acts on a command while the
interval the adversary measures stays fixed, and the adversary cannot subtract a known offset to
recover either quantity. As in the read lane, the schedule requires the response of the relay to
reach the switch before $\hat{e}_{\mathrm{or}}$, i.e., within $D$ of the request, with the command
hold $J$ included.

\subsection{Overhead Argument}
\label{sec:design:overhead}

Obfuscation is only useful if an operator can afford it. We now argue why the framework introduces
negligible overhead (RO3) in any deployment.

\textbf{Bandwidth Overhead.} As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:design}, the framework forwards the
packets it receives at their original size. The blocker packets that sustain a hold recirculate on
internal loopback ports and never reach a cable (Section~\ref{sec:impl}). Consequently, the
framework adds no frame and no byte to either link. Obfuscation that pads packets or injects dummy
ones pays a bandwidth cost that this design does not
have~\cite{ditto2022, dyer2012}.

\textbf{Latency Bound.} We delay two packets per transaction. The acknowledgment leaves at
$t_0 + D_A + \varepsilon_A$. A response that satisfies~(\ref{eq:availability}) leaves at
$t_0 + D + \varepsilon_R$, so its added delay, $D - (t_R - t_0) + \varepsilon_R$, never exceeds
$D + \varepsilon_R$ and shrinks as the outstation answers more slowly. A response that
violates~(\ref{eq:availability}) leaves as soon as its queue serves it. The operator therefore
bounds every added delay by choosing the release budget $D$, and the release tail is the only term
the operator does not set.

\textbf{Protocol Timers.} Three timers of the two endpoints can observe these delays. The TCP stack
of the master retransmits a request whose acknowledgment does not arrive within its retransmission
timeout $\tau_m$. The TCP stack of the outstation retransmits a response the master does not
acknowledge within its own timeout $\tau_o$. The relay cancels a selected control point whose
OPERATE does not follow within the select-to-operate timeout $\tau_s$, 1.0~s by default on the
SEL-751A~\cite{sel751a}. The acknowledgment hold delays the first, the response
hold delays the second, and the holds on the SELECT response and on the OPERATE command delay the
third. Consequently, the framework triggers none of them as long as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:timers}
D + J_{\max} + \varepsilon < \min(\tau_m, \tau_o, \tau_s) ,
\end{equation}
where $J_{\max}$ is the largest command hold. Condition~(\ref{eq:timers}) holds for any deployment
whose outstation answers well within its timers, because the budget only needs to cover the
response time of the outstation. RFC~6298 recommends a lower bound of one second on the
retransmission timeout~\cite{rfc6298}. On our testbed, the master
repeats an unacknowledged request after 200.8~ms and the relay repeats an unacknowledged response
after 2.969~s, while $D + J_{\max}$ is 40~ms and a 28~ms budget covers 99.94\% of the read-lane
responses of the relay (Section~\ref{sec:eval:perf}).

\textbf{Security-Performance Trade-off Argument.} The release budget $D$ sets both sides of a
trade-off. A larger budget places more of the responses of the outstation on the schedule, so fewer
late responses carry their own timing, and it adds more delay to every transaction. Within a fixed
budget, the split between $D_A$ and the configured $\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$ costs nothing, and
the operator can place the released interval anywhere below $D$ without changing the delay. We set
$\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$ to a constant, because a constant removes the variation of the device
entirely and an operator can state and check it. The schedule can carry any other value, e.g., one
drawn from a distribution, without change.
