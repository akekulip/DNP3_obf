\section{Framework Design}
\label{sec:design}

In Figure~\ref{fig:design}, we present the design. A DNP3 request enters the switch from the
master. The switch classifies it by function code, records the state it needs to recognize the
outstation's answer, forwards the request, and arms release deadlines for the packets that will
come back. When the outstation's acknowledgment and response arrive, the switch holds each in a
queue and releases it at its deadline, so the master sees a timing that the policy sets rather
than the one the outstation produces. One framework handles both monitoring and control
transactions; what differs between them is the rule that sets the deadlines. The rest of this
section presents the classification and state, the two release rules and why they differ, the
scheduling that realizes a deadline in the data plane, and the behavior for traffic the framework
does not treat.

\textbf{Classification and transaction state.} The switch parses the DNP3 link and application
headers of every packet on the protected session. It classifies each packet as a request (READ,
SELECT or OPERATE, by function code), an acknowledgment (the outstation's first segment with the
TCP ACK flag after a request), a response (a DNP3 application frame from the outstation), or
other. A
small set of registers holds the state of the one transaction in flight on the session: an
active-transaction tag, the expected acknowledgment number of the outstation's reply, the
armed deadlines, and, for control, the identity of the held OPERATE. The state is written once
per event and read by the packets that follow, so a duplicate acknowledgment or a retransmitted
request cannot arm a second deadline.

\textbf{Read-path rule: pin the acknowledgment-to-response interval.} For a READ or a SELECT,
the observable to remove is the CLRT, the interval between the acknowledgment and the response.
Let $t_A$ be the time the outstation's acknowledgment reaches the switch. The framework holds the
acknowledgment and the response and releases them at
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:read}
t_{\mathrm{ack}} = t_A + D_A, \qquad t_{\mathrm{resp}} = t_A + D_A + D_R ,
\end{equation}
where $D_A$ and $D_R$ are configured offsets. The interval the observer measures is then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:clrt}
\mathrm{CLRT} = t_{\mathrm{resp}} - t_{\mathrm{ack}} = D_R ,
\end{equation}
a policy value, in place of the native interval that the outstation's processing produces.
Equation~(\ref{eq:clrt}) holds whenever the response has arrived by $t_A + D_A + D_R$, so
$D_A + D_R$ must exceed the largest CLRT the outstation produces on its own for the traffic to be protected; every response
in our captures met the deadline. The request-to-acknowledgment interval becomes the outstation's
own acknowledgment latency plus $D_A$: it is shifted by a constant, and the sub-millisecond spread
of the outstation's TCP stack remains in it. The framework pins the CLRT; it does not claim to
remove every timing feature of the exchange, and Section~\ref{sec:eval} reports the
request-to-acknowledgment interval as measured.

\textbf{Control-path rule: anchor to the request.} An OPERATE is different, because the framework
also holds the command itself. Holding the OPERATE before it reaches the relay separates the
moment the master sent it from the moment the relay acts on it, which is what an operator may want
for a control command. But the outstation's acknowledgment then exists only after the release.
A rule anchored to that acknowledgment, as in~(\ref{eq:read}), would carry the length of the
hold into the observable. The framework therefore anchors the control path to the request. Let
$T_0$ be the time the OPERATE reaches the switch. The switch forwards the OPERATE to the relay at
$T_0 + J$, where $J$ is a configured hold, and releases the acknowledgment and the echo the master
sees at
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:control}
t_{\mathrm{ack}} = T_0 + A, \qquad t_{\mathrm{echo}} = T_0 + R ,
\end{equation}
with $A$ and $R$ configured so that $A$ exceeds $J$ plus the outstation's acknowledgment latency,
which the control plane checks before it installs the values. The master-visible OPERATE ACK-to-echo interval
is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:optime}
O = t_{\mathrm{echo}} - t_{\mathrm{ack}} = R - A ,
\end{equation}
in which $J$ does not appear. An observer that subtracts the two timestamps it has learns the
policy, not the hold, and cannot recover the device's own timing by subtracting a known
offset. $J$ is drawn per transaction from a configured set, so that the relay-facing release time
varies while the master-facing observable does not. In our experiments $D_R = R - A = 4$~ms, so
both case studies present the same 4~ms policy value to the observer.

\textbf{Security argument.} Two anchors are used because the two paths expose different
information. On the read path the request is forwarded at once and only the reply is held, so
anchoring to the outstation's acknowledgment costs nothing and lets the hold start as late as
possible; the CLRT becomes $D_R$ regardless of how long the outstation took, by~(\ref{eq:clrt}).
On the control path the command is held, so the acknowledgment is a function of $J$, and only a
request-anchored rule keeps $J$ out of the observable, by~(\ref{eq:optime}). In both cases the
device's own processing time is replaced by a constant the operator chooses.

\textbf{Reservoirs as data-plane timers.} A switch pipeline has no general-purpose timer that can
release a queued packet at a chosen time. To realize a deadline from scheduling alone, a held
packet waits in its queue behind a reservoir of small blocker packets that sit in a higher-priority
queue on the same port. Because a strict-priority scheduler drains the blockers first, the held
packet cannot leave until the reservoir has drained. The reservoir is filled by the switch's
on-chip packet generator when the request arrives, and its size sets the hold. If one blocker
drains in $\tau$, a reservoir of $K$ blockers holds a packet for about $K\tau$, so the control
plane sizes $K$ for each deadline. The generator never sends a packet toward the master or the
relay; blockers circulate on internal ports and are dropped when they expire. A tag in each
blocker names the transaction and the lane it belongs to, so a stale blocker from an earlier
transaction cannot delay a later one.

\textbf{Two independent lanes.} A READ and an OPERATE can be in flight at the same time, and a
single strict-priority schedule would serialize their reservoirs: whichever held longer would
delay the other and move its release off its deadline. The framework therefore gives the read
treatment and the control treatment their own internal recirculation ports and queues, so the two
holds proceed independently. The register state of the two lanes is likewise separate, so a
control-path packet never touches read-path state.

\textbf{Unmatched transactions and failing open.} A defense on a control path must not become an
availability risk. A packet that does not belong to a protected session, a request the framework
does not recognize, an acknowledgment with no armed transaction, or a response that arrives after
its transaction has retired is forwarded unchanged. Consequently, an unexpected exchange loses its
protection but not its delivery. Every hold is also bounded. A fail-open budget on the number of
blocker passes releases a held packet if its reservoir has not drained within a configured
horizon and clears the transaction state, so a lost acknowledgment or response cannot leave a
deadline armed forever. With the timing mode switched off, the same program forwards every packet
immediately and arms nothing.

\textbf{Byte preservation.} The framework only holds and forwards packets. It neither pads a
response, injects a packet into the protected exchange, nor edits a DNP3 field, so every byte the
master reassembles is the byte the relay produced, and the payload's cyclic redundancy checks
remain valid.
