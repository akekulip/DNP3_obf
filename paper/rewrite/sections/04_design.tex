\section{Design}
\label{sec:design}

In this section, we first describe the timing model and the packet flow through the framework.
Then we define the design parameters and the conditions under which the switch can follow the
release schedule. Last, we explain how queues and the P4 pipeline realize this schedule.

\subsection{Timing Model and Packet Flow}
\label{sec:design:overview}

To change the timing an adversary observes, we place a programmable switch between the master and
the outstation. As a READ poll or the SELECT phase passes through the switch, we forward the
request and hold the returning acknowledgment and response, which carry the timing seen by the adversary. For OPERATE, it also holds the command
before forwarding it to the relay. Because both cases use the request's arrival to schedule the returning packets, the release
deadlines do not follow the outstation's acknowledgment latency.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{figures/model/fig_m01_release_timeline}
  \caption{Release timeline of one READ transaction. All five instants are at the switch: the
  request arrives at $t_0$, the acknowledgment and response arrive at $t_A$ and $t_R$, and the
  switch releases them at $e_A$ and $e_R$. The schedule is anchored at $t_0$. (a) The response
  arrives before its deadline. (b) It arrives late and leaves when its queue next serves it.
  The drawing illustrates the ideal schedule; actual release also includes queue service.}
  \label{fig:timeline}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Timing Model.} In Figure~\ref{fig:timeline}, we show the timing of a READ transaction.
We denote the request's arrival at the switch by $t_0$, the acknowledgment and response arrivals
by $t_A$ and $t_R$, and their departures toward the master by $e_A$ and $e_R$. At this switch
boundary, the original acknowledgment-to-response interval is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:clrt-original}
\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{original}} = t_R - t_A .
\end{equation}
Holding the two packets changes that interval to
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:clrt-new}
e_R-e_A = \mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{original}}+(e_R-t_R)-(e_A-t_A),
\end{equation}
where $e_A-t_A$ and $e_R-t_R$ are the acknowledgment and response holds. The adversary measures CLRT on the master-facing link, where the difference in path and capture
delay between the two packets also contributes to the interval. We distinguish that measurement
from the switch-side interval when interpreting the results.

\textbf{Replacing the Interval.} Because a constant hold on each packet shifts the original CLRT by the difference between the
holds, it preserves the variation that a timing classifier can use. To absorb that variation, we choose release deadlines from the request, such that a packet arriving
early waits longer and one arriving closer to its deadline waits less. This choice requires
both packets to arrive before their respective deadlines, which constrains the parameters below.

\textbf{Holding with Queues.} To keep a packet waiting, we place it in a queue below another queue
of higher priority. The higher queue contains internal \emph{blocker packets}, which the scheduler serves instead of
the held packet for as long as the higher queue remains nonempty. We circulate the
blockers until their deadline, after which they stop returning. After the remaining blockers drain, the scheduler can serve the held packet. In this way, the
queues provide the wait, while the P4 program decides which packets enter them and when blockers stop.

\subsection{Design Parameters and Constraints}
\label{sec:design:read}

\textbf{Release Deadlines.} We use two configured parameters: $D_A$, the acknowledgment deadline
measured from the request, and the configured $\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$, the desired gap
between the acknowledgment and response. When a request arrives, we set
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:schedule}
\hat{e}_A=t_0+D_A, \qquad
\hat{e}_R=t_0+D_A+\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}.
\end{equation}
The hats denote scheduled instants, not measured departures. We call their combined offset
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:budget}
D=D_A+\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}
\end{equation}
the response \emph{release budget}. For example, the evaluated configuration uses $D_A=20$~ms and
a configured CLRT of 8~ms, giving $D=28$~ms. Note that $D_A$ starts at the request, so part of this interval has already elapsed when the
acknowledgment reaches the switch; its hold is only the remaining wait.

\textbf{Per-Transaction Policies.} A fixed policy uses the same offsets on every admitted request.
We can also select a pair of offsets per request from a configured distribution. The selection does
not use the operation code, and the chosen offsets remain fixed for that transaction. The
availability conditions below apply to every selected pair.

\textbf{Packet Availability.} Because the switch cannot release a packet it has not received,
the ideal schedule requires both
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:availability}
t_A\leq t_0+D_A, \qquad t_R\leq t_0+D.
\end{equation}
Under these conditions, the ideal holds are
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:coupling}
\begin{aligned}
\hat{e}_A-t_A &= D_A-(t_A-t_0),\\
\hat{e}_R-t_R &= D-(t_R-t_0).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Consequently, the holds absorb changes in arrival time without moving the scheduled departures.
If either packet arrives late, its release depends on when it arrives and when the queue can serve
it. To follow both deadlines, we must admit the transaction, sustain each hold with enough blockers,
and receive the acknowledgment before its earlier deadline, even when the response meets its budget.

\textbf{Actual Release.} For an admitted transaction whose packets arrive in time and whose holds
last until their deadlines, write $e_A=\hat{e}_A+\varepsilon_A$ and
$e_R=\hat{e}_R+\varepsilon_R$. These release tails include the time for remaining blockers to drain
and for the held packets to be served and forwarded. Then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:released}
\begin{aligned}
e_A-t_0 &= D_A+\varepsilon_A,\\
e_R-e_A &= \mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}+\varepsilon_R-\varepsilon_A.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Consequently, the actual gap differs from its configured value by the difference between two
release tails, neither of which the operator directly sets. Section~\ref{sec:eval:tail} evaluates the
master-facing excess over the ACK deadline, which also includes path and capture effects.

\textbf{Security Argument.} Equation~(\ref{eq:released}) removes the explicit arrival-time terms
from the scheduled intervals. If the residual tails and path effects are independent of device
execution, the released intervals no longer expose that execution time. However, this independence is an assumption to examine: queue state, late arrivals and an exhausted
blocker budget can affect release, even though the deadlines themselves are request-anchored. We therefore evaluate both the concentration around the configured
interval and the timing information retained by fixed and adaptive classifiers
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:ro2}). The argument motivates applying the schedule to other devices, but
one device cannot establish that different device models become indistinguishable.

\subsubsection{Control Transactions}
\label{sec:design:control}

To separate the forwarding of an OPERATE from its arrival at the switch, we also configure a set
of command holds $J$. When the OPERATE arrives at $T_0$, the switch selects a hold and schedules
forwarding toward the relay at $T_0+J$. Actual forwarding can trail this instant because of queue
service. We configure the returning acknowledgment and response with the same offsets as the read
lane:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:control}
\hat{e}_{\mathrm{ack}}=T_0+D_A, \qquad
\hat{e}_{\mathrm{or}}=T_0+D_A+\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}.
\end{equation}
Because both packets must reach the switch before these deadlines, their availability must account
for the command hold and the relay's work after receiving the command, unlike a READ. Consequently, the
largest admissible $J$ must leave time for both the acknowledgment and response, not just for the
response to meet $D$.

\textbf{Security Argument.} Under the same scheduling conditions, the released OPERATE interval
is $O=e_{\mathrm{or}}-e_{\mathrm{ack}}=\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}+
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{or}}-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ack}}$ at the switch boundary. The command hold does
not appear explicitly in this expression. As such, we can configure different command holds under one return schedule, provided the packets
are available in time and the residual release effects remain acceptable. The campaign
records the configured set $\{2,6,12\}$~ms and the intervals observed at the master; it does not
measure the selected $J$ or physical actuation on each exchange.

\subsubsection{Latency and Timer Constraints}
\label{sec:design:overhead}

\textbf{Delay and Coverage.} A larger response budget can accommodate slower responses, but it
also increases the scheduled request-to-response interval. For an on-time response released after
its deadline, the hold is $D-(t_R-t_0)+\varepsilon_R$, which is at most $D+\varepsilon_R$.
This is a bound on its hold given a release tail, not a bound on every measured transaction.
Changing the split between $D_A$ and the configured CLRT keeps the response deadline fixed, but
only splits that satisfy both availability conditions are usable. We evaluate this trade-off in
Section~\ref{sec:eval:policy}.

\textbf{Protocol Timers.} Three endpoint timers constrain the settings. First, the master waits for a TCP acknowledgment before retransmitting its request. Second, the
outstation waits for acknowledgment of its response. Last, the relay waits for OPERATE after
accepting SELECT. The last interval includes
the wait for the SELECT response, the master's next request, and the OPERATE hold. To choose a
policy, the operator must leave room for these existing delays, network transit and release tails
within each timer. These margins depend on the deployment, rather than on the configured budget alone.

RFC~6298 recommends a one-second lower bound on the retransmission timeout~\cite{rfc6298}, but we
check endpoint behavior rather than assume that recommendation is enforced. The SEL-751A specifies
a default select-to-operate timeout of 1.0~s~\cite{sel751a}. Section~\ref{sec:eval:timers} reports the
observed transaction waits and the separate retransmission tests. Those measurements qualify the
settings on this testbed; they do not guarantee negligible delay in every deployment.

\textbf{Supported Holding Range.} Blockers have a finite pass budget, so a configured deadline
can exceed the hold that the implementation sustains. The control plane uses an estimated horizon
$H$ to restrict admission, and the policy sweep checks where the measured release stops following
the configured deadline. Because this horizon is estimated rather than measured as a worst-case bound, we also check the
supported range experimentally; settings beyond it may release packets early and lose timing protection.

\subsection{Queue and P4 Realization}
\label{sec:design:pipeline}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig_design}
\caption{Packet flow and its mapping to the P4 pipeline. Ingress classifies packets, arms
  request-anchored deadlines and selects queues; the traffic manager provides the wait.
  ACK and response queues share one strict-priority scheduler, while OPERATE requests use
  a separate scheduler. Internal loopback bypasses egress. Returning blockers are re-enqueued
  or dropped according to expiry; after the higher-priority queues drain, the released packet
  returns to ingress and follows the normal output path. Scheduled deadlines do not include
  the residual release tail.}
\label{fig:design}
\end{figure*}

In Figure~\ref{fig:design}, we map the queueing idea to the switch pipeline. The parser exposes the fields used to identify a packet, based on which ingress chooses whether to
forward it, hold it or process it as an internal blocker. The traffic manager stores these packets
and selects which queue to serve.
Consequently, the wait occurs in the queues, while the P4 program runs to completion on each arrival.

\textbf{Ingress Decisions.} To protect a fresh transaction, ingress records its identity and
writes the request-anchored deadlines. Ingress directs the returning acknowledgment and response to their held queues, while an internal
blocker is re-enqueued or dropped according to its identity, deadline and remaining pass budget. A released real packet instead takes the normal
forwarding path. The evaluated implementation keeps one protected transaction in flight; a second
request that cannot be admitted is forwarded without this timing protection.

\textbf{Queue Scheduling.} The ACK blocker, held ACK, response blocker and held response occupy
four queues, in descending priority, on one internal port. Consequently, the response and ACK queues share a scheduler. OPERATE uses a separate internal port, with its blocker queue above
its held-command queue, so ACK traffic cannot starve the command. Strict priority provides the
hold only while the relevant higher-priority queues remain nonempty~\cite{sppifo2020}.

\textbf{Loopback and Output.} Packets served from either internal port bypass egress and return
to ingress. This path lets blockers repeat their deadline checks without reaching an external link.
After release, a real packet returns to ingress once and is forwarded through normal egress toward
the master or outstation. Internal blockers add no traffic to those external links. Other
obfuscation mechanisms use external traffic for their own objectives~\cite{ditto2022, dyer2012};
the waiting mechanism here uses the switch's internal queues. The implementation details and the
handling of retransmitted commands are described in Sections~\ref{sec:impl} and
\ref{sec:eval:limits}.
