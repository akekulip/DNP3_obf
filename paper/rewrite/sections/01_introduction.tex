\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Device fingerprinting has been an essential step in cyber reconnaissance, allowing adversaries
to reveal unique features of target networks and to design effective, stealthy attack
strategies. These techniques are becoming increasingly critical in industrial control systems
(ICSs) such as power grids, where adversaries often use IP-based control networks and computing
devices within as entry points to inflict physical damage. Consequently, fingerprinting shifts
the focus from visited websites and user biometric behavior to device models and types of
control operations that are critical to ICS attacks. In the 2015 attack that disrupted Ukrainian
power grids and the Stuxnet attack that disrupted Iranian nuclear power facilities, it is widely
believed that adversaries stayed in their systems for at least 6 months to perform cyber
reconnaissance~\cite{ukraine2015, stuxnet2011}.

To disrupt device fingerprinting, many studies have presented network traffic
obfuscation~\cite{wright2009, dyer2012}. Device
fingerprinting targeting general computing environments generally relies on network-level
features such as packet size and/or inter-packet latency observed from communication
patterns~\cite{sirinam2018}. Consequently, existing traffic
obfuscation often focuses on (i) padding and splitting network packets, which hide or change the
distribution of network packet sizes~\cite{wright2009, walkietalkie2017, panchenko2016}, and (ii)
delaying network packets and adding dummy ones, which disrupt the inter-packet timing
pattern~\cite{dyer2012, tamaraw2014, wang2008dlp, juarez2016}. Since manipulating
communication networks can introduce runtime overhead, recent works have begun to offload
traffic obfuscation onto programmable network switches, which change communication patterns at
much higher line rates than CPUs~\cite{ditto2022, minos2025, securitas2026}.

Unfortunately, these methods cannot be directly applied to ICS environments for two main
reasons. First, device fingerprinting methods on ICS devices rely on different features from the
ones in general computing environments. Instead of using network layer knowledge, they attempt
to use behavior on physical devices to reveal device model or types of operations. For example,
work in~\cite{formby2016, shen2018, ahmed2021, pospisil2023} uses the latency between the
TCP acknowledge packets and the actual response from the same device to estimate execution time
there. Since each
vendors may choose different materials or algorithms to implement the same control
functionality, this time can be accurate in fingerprinting those devices. Second, existing
traffic obfuscation is performed over encrypted channels, which are necessary to mix the
obfuscated and normal traffic. Unfortunately, ICS networks still rely on unencrypted traffic due
to a wide range of legacy devices, despite ICS network protocols may provide security features.
Directly padding or adding dummy packets can easily reveal obfuscated packets, exposing the the
underlying traffic pattern to adversaries.

In this paper, we propose an in-network timing-obfuscation framework for ICS traffic. Instead of
padding packets or adding dummy ones, the framework changes only when the packets of a transaction
leave the network. It schedules the acknowledgment and response from the request, so packets
that arrive in time can follow an operator-configured interval, subject to release effects.

\subsection{Contributions}

To overcome the two drawbacks above, we design a framework that runs on a programmable switch placed
in front of an outstation. The switch holds the transport acknowledgment and the application
response of the outstation, and it releases each of them at a deadline computed from the moment the
request arrives. Because both deadlines are computed from the request, their scheduled values do not depend on
when the outstation acknowledges or answers. Actual release also depends on packet availability
and queue service. The framework works with readable protocol traffic by holding existing packets
rather than requiring endpoint encryption.

\textbf{The Framework's Role.} We position the framework as a complement to the protections an ICS
already runs, such as intrusion detection. The framework cannot hide which operation an exchange
carries, because DNP3 leaves the function code readable, and the release budget controls the scheduled delay of
protected exchanges. It leaves the application payload unchanged and requires no
modification of the master, the outstation or the DNP3 protocol. Consequently, it can protect legacy
devices that can neither be patched nor replaced, which are the devices that fingerprinting targets.

Even though we present the framework for DNP3, the design concept can be extended to other ICS
protocols. The scheduling idea requires an identifiable request and returning acknowledgment and response.
Applying it to another protocol would also require parsing, transaction matching and timer checks
for that protocol, which we do not evaluate here.

The main contributions of the paper are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Replace arrival-dependent timing with scheduled intervals.} We design a release
    schedule that holds real protocol packets and computes both release deadlines from the arrival
    of the request. The operator sets the scheduled request-to-acknowledgment interval and CLRT, while
    packet availability and residual release effects constrain how closely actual timing follows.
  \item \textbf{Obfuscate plaintext ICS traffic without adding a packet.} The framework only delays
    packets and requires no endpoint encryption. We relate the release budget to packet
    availability and protocol timers, then evaluate the delay and observed timer behavior.
  \item \textbf{Implement the framework at line rate.} On an Intel Tofino-1 switch, we hold each
    packet behind a strict-priority queue of blocker packets the switch generates. The queues sustain the wait while P4 checks deadlines on returning blockers. We cover both the read and the control
    transaction of DNP3.
\end{itemize}

For evaluation, we placed the switch between a DNP3 master and a physical SEL-751A feeder protection
relay and collected 63{,}360 exchanges in 22 paired runs, each containing both cases.
We recorded packet timestamps at the master-facing link, and we tested it against (i) a classifier trained before
the framework was deployed, (ii) an adaptive adversary that retrains on obfuscated traffic, and
(iii) an adaptive adversary that pools many exchanges of the same operation into one signature, as
Formby et al.\ do.

Our experimental results show reduced timing variation and fixed-classifier accuracy, together
with the latency cost and residual information:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The released interval settles on the configured value. The medians of READ, SELECT and
    OPERATE move from 2.121, 2.041 and 2.929~ms to 8.005, 8.005 and 8.000~ms, and the interquartile
    range of READ falls from 2.772 to 0.022~ms. The operator can place the interval anywhere from
    2 to 26~ms within a fixed budget, and the measured median follows each tested setting to within 6~$\mu$s.
  \item Timing-based separation decreases. A classifier that separates READ and SELECT at 0.785 balanced accuracy
    under Timing OFF is at 0.507 under the framework, against a chance level of 0.5. A classifier
    trained before deployment falls from 0.803 to 0.333 on the three operations, i.e., chance. An
    adaptive adversary reaches 0.445, and the residual tests find timing information associated
    with request spacing without isolating its cause.
  \item The latency cost is measured. Median READ request-to-response time rises from 2.681 to
    28.107~ms. No TCP retransmission was observed, and the longest request-to-ACK wait was
    20.19~ms, compared with the master's separately observed 200.8~ms retransmission interval.
\end{itemize}
