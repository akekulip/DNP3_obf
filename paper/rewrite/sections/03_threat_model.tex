\section{Threat Model and Objectives}
\label{sec:threat}

In this section, we first describe the adversary we consider and the knowledge it seeks. Then we
specify the three research objectives the framework must achieve, RO1 to RO3, and the scope of our
work.

\subsection{Threat Model}
\label{sec:threat:model}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\IfFileExists{figures/fig_observation.pdf}{\includegraphics{figures/fig_observation}}{\figplaceholder{Observation model: master, observed master-facing link, programmable switch, relay-facing link (not observed), SEL-751A outstation.}}
\caption{Observation model. The adversary monitors the shaded master-facing link. The dashed paths
  are not visible to it.}
\label{fig:observation}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Assumptions on Adversary's Capability.} We assume that an adversary has penetrated the
control network and passively monitors the master-facing link, the position from which Formby et
al.\ fingerprint outstations~\cite{formby2016}, as shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:observation}. The adversary is able to record the headers, the size and the arrival
time of every packet. Because DNP3 carries no transport security by
default~\cite{east2009}, the adversary also reads the function code of every
request and can label each exchange by its operation, e.g., a READ poll or the SELECT and OPERATE
of a control command. We assume that the adversary knows the framework and the interval it
enforces, but cannot read the internal state of the switch or monitor the link between the switch
and the outstation. This is a practical assumption, as the switch sits at the edge of the
outstation, and the relay-facing link can be a single cable between two devices in the same
substation cabinet.

\textbf{Adversary's Goal.} The adversary aims at fingerprinting the outstation from the timing of
its exchanges, and from that timing it infers the make and model of the device and the operation
each exchange performs. Based on this knowledge, the adversary can design targeted attacks, e.g.,
false data injection that must match the devices it compromises~\cite{liufdia2009}.
We consider two adversaries. A \emph{fixed} adversary builds its classifier from traffic captured
before the framework is deployed and applies it afterwards. An \emph{adaptive} adversary collects
traffic after deployment, labels it with the plaintext function codes and retrains its classifier.
The adaptive adversary can also pool many exchanges of the same operation into one signature, as
Formby et al.\ do~\cite{formby2016}.

\subsection{Research Objectives}
\label{sec:threat:ro}

\textbf{The Framework's Objective.} We aim at disrupting timing-based fingerprinting of an
outstation without changing the outstation, the master or the DNP3 protocol.
We specify three research objectives (RO) as follows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{RO1:} for both the read poll and the select-before-operate control, we aim at
    making the CLRT the adversary measures a value the operator configures, independent of the
    execution time of the outstation.
  \item \textbf{RO2:} for fixed and adaptive adversaries, we aim at removing the execution time of
    the outstation from everything they learn from the timing of an exchange, such that a fixed
    adversary performs no better than chance and whatever an adaptive adversary still learns
    reflects the switch rather than the device.
  \item \textbf{RO3:} for the operation of the control network, we aim to keep added delay within
    the endpoint timer margins without adding packets or bytes to the external exchange. We
    measure the latency cost and check for retransmissions and refused control operations under
    the evaluated settings; these observations do not establish a timer guarantee for other deployments.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Trusted Components.} To achieve these objectives, we add only the switch and its control
plane to the trusted computing base. The control plane installs the release policy and reads it
back, and the switch enforces it. The framework needs no cooperation from the master or the
outstation, and neither of them is modified.

\textbf{For Payload-Based Fingerprinting.} We designed the framework to hide timing, not content.
It forwards every packet at its original size and leaves the function codes readable, so the
adversary still knows which operation an exchange carries. The framework aims to suppress the execution-time variation carried by the released intervals. The physical operation time of Definition 2 is a second feature, and the estimate Formby et al.\ retain reads a timestamp inside the payload rather than the timing of a
packet~\cite{formby2016}. Such content is a different problem, and payload
protection is complementary to the framework. We leave the combination of the two to future work.

\textbf{For Active Adversaries.} An adversary that injects, delays or drops packets, or that
compromises the switch or the outstation, performs attacks beyond reconnaissance and is outside this
model. Such an adversary still faces existing defenses, e.g., specification-based intrusion
detection for DNP3~\cite{linbro2013}, and the framework leaves every byte those
defenses inspect unchanged.
