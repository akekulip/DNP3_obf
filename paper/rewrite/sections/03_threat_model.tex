\section{Threat Model and Research Objectives}
\label{sec:threat}

\caption{Observation model. The master reaches the SEL-751A outstation through one programmable

the switch-internal scheduling lanes (dashed) are not observed, by the adversary or by our

\label{fig:observation}

\textbf{The observer.} We consider a passive observer on the master-facing communication path
between the DNP3 master and the outstation, as in the fingerprinting attack of Formby et
al.~\cite{formbyWhosControlYour2016}. Figure~\ref{fig:observation} shows the setup. The observer
captures every packet on that link, reads directions, timestamps and packet lengths, and, because
legacy DNP3 usually runs without transport security across substation
networks~\cite{eastTaxonomyAttacksDNP32009}, reads the DNP3 function codes in the plaintext.
From these it derives the transaction timing of Section~\ref{sec:bg} and can train a classifier
that separates transaction classes from timing features. The observer cannot modify or inject
packets, compromise the master or the outstation, control the switch, or read the switch's
internal state. It does not observe the relay-facing link, because in our deployment that link
is inside the switch and no tap exists; and it cannot see physical breaker motion. An observer that
sends its own probes and an active adversary that changes the network are out of scope; the paper
stays with passive observation, the setting that prior traffic-obfuscation work targets.

\textbf{What the observer wants.} The observer fingerprints the outstation to prepare a later
attack. Because a control-related attack such as false-data injection works only when the
attacker knows the target device~\cite{liuFalseDataInjection2009}, this reconnaissance is the
enabling step. Two timing features are in scope, following Formby et
al.~\cite{formbyWhosControlYour2016}: the CLRT of a READ and of the SELECT phase of SBO, and the
master-visible operation time of an OPERATE, i.e., the interval between its acknowledgment and
its echo. The observer also sees the request-to-acknowledgment interval; we report it because it
is available to the observer, and we state what the framework does and does not do to it.

\textbf{Research objectives.} Our objective is to remove the device's own timing from the
master-facing observation. We state it as three objectives that the design targets and the
evaluation answers one by one.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{RO1 (read timing).} Normalize the CLRT of READ and of the SELECT phase of SBO to a
    configured policy value, so that it no longer reflects the outstation's internal processing.
  \item \textbf{RO2 (control timing).} Keep the master-visible acknowledgment-to-echo interval of
    an OPERATE at a configured policy value that is independent of the switch-internal hold
    applied to the command.
  \item \textbf{RO3 (timing leakage).} Reduce the information about the transaction class that the
    measured timing features carry, so that a classifier trained on Timing OFF timing features performs no
    better than chance on Obfuscated ones.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{What is out of scope.} The framework does not hide the function codes, which stay
visible in plaintext DNP3; RO3 is about the timing channel, not the command semantics. It does not
change packet sizes, and no size-related claim is made in this paper. It does not claim that two
different devices become indistinguishable; the evaluation has one outstation, and what it shows
is that this outstation's timing signature is replaced by a policy signature.
