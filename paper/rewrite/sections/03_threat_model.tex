\section{Threat Model and Objectives}
\label{sec:threat}

In this section, we first describe the adversary we consider and the knowledge it seeks. Then we
specify the three research objectives the framework must achieve, RO1 to RO3, and the attacks it
does not address.

\subsection{Threat Model}
\label{sec:threat:model}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering

\caption{Observation model. The adversary monitors the master-facing link (shaded). The
  relay-facing link and the lanes inside the switch (dashed) are not visible to the adversary.}
\label{fig:observation}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Assumptions on Adversary's Capability.} We assume that an adversary has penetrated the
control network and passively monitors the master-facing link, the position from which Formby et
al.\ fingerprint outstations~\cite{formbyWhosControlYour2016}, as shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:observation}. We assume that the adversary records the headers, the size and the
arrival time of every packet. Because DNP3 carries no transport security by
default~\cite{eastTaxonomyAttacksDNP32009}, the adversary also reads the function code of every
request and can label each exchange by its operation, e.g., a READ poll or the SELECT and OPERATE
of a control command. We assume that the adversary knows the framework and the interval it
enforces, but cannot read the internal state of the switch or monitor the link between the switch
and the outstation. We argue that this is a practical assumption, as the switch sits at the edge
of the outstation, and the relay-facing link can be a single cable between two devices in the same
substation cabinet.

\textbf{Adversary's Goal.} The adversary aims at fingerprinting the outstation from the timing of
its exchanges, and from that timing it infers the device type and the operation each exchange
performs. Based on this knowledge, the adversary can design targeted attacks, e.g., false data
injection that must match the devices it compromises~\cite{liuFalseDataInjection2009}. We consider two adversaries. A
\emph{fixed} adversary builds its classifier from traffic captured before the framework is deployed
and applies it afterwards. An \emph{adaptive} adversary collects traffic after deployment, labels
it with the plaintext function codes and retrains its classifier. The adaptive adversary can also
pool many exchanges of the same operation into one signature, as Formby et al.\
do~\cite{formbyWhosControlYour2016}.

\subsection{Research Objectives}
\label{sec:threat:ro}

\textbf{The Framework's Objective.} We aim at disrupting timing-based fingerprinting of an
outstation without changing the outstation, the master or the DNP3 protocol.
We specify three research objectives (RO) as follows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{RO1:} for both the read poll and the select-before-operate control, we aim at
    making the CLRT the adversary measures a value the operator configures, independent of the
    execution time of the outstation.
  \item \textbf{RO2:} for fixed and adaptive adversaries, we aim at removing the information the
    CLRT carries about the outstation, such that their classifiers perform no better than chance,
    even when they pool many exchanges.
  \item \textbf{RO3:} for the operation of the control network, we aim at negligible overhead. The
    framework adds no packet and no byte, and it keeps every added delay within the timers of TCP
    and DNP3, such that no request is retransmitted and no control operation is refused.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{For Payload-Based Fingerprinting.} We designed the framework to hide timing, not content.
It forwards every packet at its original size and leaves the function codes readable, so the
adversary still knows which operation an exchange carries. What the framework removes is how long the device
takes to perform it. Formby et al.\ also estimate the physical operation time of a breaker from an
event timestamp inside the response payload~\cite{formbyWhosControlYour2016}. Such content is a different problem, and payload protection
is complementary to the framework. We leave the combination of the two to future work.

\textbf{For Active Adversaries.} An adversary that injects, delays or drops packets, or that
compromises the switch or the outstation, performs attacks beyond reconnaissance and is outside this
model. Such an adversary still faces existing defenses, e.g., specification-based intrusion
detection for DNP3~\cite{linAdaptingBroSCADA2013}, and the framework leaves every byte those
defenses inspect unchanged.
