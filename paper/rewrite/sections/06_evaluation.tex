\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{What we measured, and where}
\label{sec:eval:testbed}

\textbf{Evaluation Cases.} We compare two arms of the same program. They differ only in the release
schedule. In \emph{Timing OFF} the switch forwards the outstation's acknowledgment and response as
they arrive; in \emph{Obfuscated} it holds them and releases them on the deadlines of
Section~\ref{sec:design}. The switch is the only path between the master and the relay, and every
timestamp is taken on the master-facing link. Consequently each interval below is one a passive
observer can record: the arrival of the transport acknowledgment to the arrival of the application
response, both on the master's own interface.

\textbf{Dataset.} We report per DNP3 exchange. The campaign holds 63{,}360 of them, half with the
mechanism running and half without, collected over five hours in interleaved blocks so that drift
cannot separate the two arms. Every request was answered, and no exchange was retransmitted,
malformed or excluded. A separate sweep on the same testbed covers 16 configured policies and
three controls with the mechanism disabled.

\subsection{Does the released interval reach its configured value? (RO1)}
\label{sec:eval:ro1}

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{figures/clrt/fig_clrt}
  \caption{Measured READ CLRT, at both scales. Columns are the arms, rows the scale. Above,
  common 1~ms bins anchored at 0~ms with a linear ordinate; finite bins are half-open and the
  category past the break holds everything at or above 15~ms, 0.409\% of Timing OFF and 0.027\%
  of Obfuscated. Below, 0.005~ms bins within 0.10~ms of the configured
  $\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$ (dashed), where the mass the upper grid divides between two
  neighbouring bins resolves into one mode. The ordinate is the percentage of that arm's entire
  sample throughout, not of the window, so the rows are comparable; the window holds 13.58\% and
  99.86\%. Above, $n$~=~26{,}400 per arm, mean 2.800 and 4.012~ms, sd 2.609 and 0.628~ms.}
  \label{fig:hist}
\end{figure}

\textbf{RO1.} The read-path schedule of~(\ref{eq:schedule}) does replace
$\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{original}}$ with the configured $\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$. We compare
the two arms over all 22 grouped runs as full empirical distributions, because Formby et
al.~\cite{formbyWhosControlYour2016} classify an outstation by the shape of that distribution.

Figure~\ref{fig:hist} shows the two READ distributions. Before obfuscation the interval spans
1.01 to 83.46~ms and stays multi-modal, with peaks near 1.2, 2.1 and 4.0~ms; after it, 99.9\% of
transactions fall within 0.10~ms of the configured $\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$. Its lower row
resolves the released interval into a narrow distribution about that value, where the Timing OFF
arm places only 13.6\% of its transactions.

The change is a replacement, not a shift. The standard deviation falls from 2.609 to 0.628~ms for
READ and from 2.267 to 0.024~ms for SELECT. Under a session-level bootstrap those give variance
ratios of 0.058 (95\% CI [0.019, 0.101]) and 0.0001
(95\% CI [1.4$\times$10$^{-5}$, 3.4$\times$10$^{-4}$]). A defense that shifted the interval would keep that spread, since translating a sample leaves its
variance unchanged. Translated onto the protected median, the Timing OFF distribution retains
standard deviations of 2.609 and 2.267~ms, four and ninety-four times what we measure.

The medians sit where the policy puts them. The median $\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{original}}$ is
2.116~ms for READ and 2.050~ms for SELECT, with interquartile ranges of 2.778 and 2.697~ms. Under
the mechanism both medians sit at the configured 4.000~ms and each interquartile range falls to
0.006~ms, two orders of magnitude below the 1~ms step between neighbouring policy settings in the
sweep of Section~\ref{sec:eval:policy}. The median alone understates the change. The Timing OFF distributions rise in a few discrete
steps, so one observation already says something about the class. The obfuscated ones become a
single step at the scheduled release, an instant computed from the acknowledgment and a fixed
offset, which no longer depends on how long the device took.
Figure~\ref{fig:hist} shows that for READ; the per-class distributions for all three, and the
per-run medians in acquisition order, are published with the artifact.

Fewer than one READ exchange in a thousand departs from the scheduled release by more than 1~ms,
the largest reaching 57.182~ms: a response that reaches the switch after its scheduled release
leaves as soon as the hold queue serves it. Those late exchanges are the coverage boundary that
Section~\ref{sec:eval:policy} counts.

\textbf{Stability within the Campaign.} The obfuscated median sits at the scheduled release in
every one of the 22 grouped runs and every class, while the Timing OFF medians stay separated, so
the effect holds in every run across the five hours. The per-run medians and
interquartile ranges in acquisition order are published with the artifact.

\subsection{Does the control lane behave the same way? (RO2)}
\label{sec:eval:ro2}

\textbf{RO2.} The control-path rule of~(\ref{eq:control}) holds the master-visible
response-to-ACK interval at the policy value. Timed from the request, its observable is
$O = \mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$, in which the internal hold $J$ does not appear. Because that
deadline is computed from the request, the read-lane coverage of
Section~\ref{sec:eval:policy} does not describe it, and the switch draws $J$ per transaction from
the configured codebook $\{2, 6, 12\}$~ms. The OPERATE median moves from 2.937~ms under
Timing OFF to 4.000~ms under the mechanism, the interquartile range falls from 2.827 to 0.006~ms,
and the interval stays concentrated at the configured value in every run.

\subsection{Can a classifier still use the replaced interval? (RO3)}
\label{sec:eval:ro3}

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{figures/ndss/fig_leakage}
  \caption{Transaction-class leakage under the two attacker models, leaving out one grouped run at
  a time. (a) Balanced accuracy by attacker condition and feature set. The abscissa names the
  training condition and the arm it is tested on: fixed on Timing OFF, the same fixed model on
  Obfuscated traffic, and a model retrained on Obfuscated traffic. Whiskers span the 22 held-out
  runs and are not a confidence interval. (b) Mutual information between the CLRT and the class,
  against a within-run permutation null.}
  \label{fig:leakage}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Setup.} Formby et al.~\cite{formbyWhosControlYour2016} classify an outstation by the
distribution of its CLRT, so we test whether that distribution still carries the class once the
switch replaces it. The classifier sees both separately measurable intervals, the CLRT and the
request-to-acknowledgment interval, and may exploit any dependence between them; we exclude their
sum so that no feature is a linear combination of the others. We estimate mutual information
between the CLRT and the class in bits against a null built by shuffling class labels within each
grouped run over 1{,}000 permutations.

\textbf{RO3.} Under Timing OFF the three classes differ in CLRT, with medians of 2.116, 2.050 and
2.937~ms, the separation Formby et al.\ exploit, while their request-to-acknowledgment medians of
0.555, 0.555 and 0.558~ms barely differ. The mechanism collapses the CLRT: all three medians sit
at 4.000~ms. A classifier trained on Timing OFF traffic reaches 0.745 balanced accuracy on held-out
Timing OFF exchanges from the CLRT alone and 0.820 from both intervals, and applied to obfuscated
traffic it falls to 0.333 in both cases, the chance level. The information behind that accuracy is
removed rather than displaced: mutual information between the CLRT and the class falls from
0.383~bits, far above its permutation null ($p = 0.001$), to 0.004~bits, inside it ($p = 0.084$),
a result that holds for any classifier reading the interval.

\textbf{Pooling Recovers Nothing.} Formby et al.\ build a signature from many observations rather
than one, so we give the adversary that advantage and more: it pools $k$ same-class exchanges,
summarizes each interval by its mean, spread and extremes, and retrains on obfuscated traffic
itself. On Timing OFF traffic, pooled exchanges make the CLRT identifying: balanced accuracy rises to
0.988 at $k = 20$. On the replaced CLRT the same adversary stays at chance. We measure 0.347 from one
exchange and 0.341 from twenty, because the replacement leaves no class information for aggregation to
accumulate.

\textbf{What Retraining Recovers.} The adversary can still learn from the request-to-acknowledgment
interval, which the mechanism does not replace. Retrained on both intervals it reaches 0.782 from
one exchange and 0.942 from five pooled ones, and the source is structure the switch adds rather
than the relay's timing. The read lane times its deadlines from the outstation's acknowledgment
and the control lane from the request, so the row-normalised confusion, published with the
artifact, identifies OPERATE in 0.940 of cases. READ and SELECT separate through
request-to-acknowledgment medians of 21.336 and 21.239~ms, a gap that appears only under the
mechanism, since the relay acknowledged both at 0.555~ms under Timing OFF. Both sources lie in the
switch's release logic, where the schedule sets them.

\subsection{What range of settings does the mechanism support? (RO4)}
\label{sec:eval:policy}

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{figures/ndss/fig_policy_coverage_cost}
  \caption{The release policy is programmable and bounded on both sides. (a) Timing OFF read-lane tail
  against the budget $D$ and the horizon $H$. (b) At $D=24$~ms the measured $\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$ follows the
  configured $\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$ while the response latency stays fixed. (c) Raising $D_A$ saturates near $H$.
  (d) End-to-end response time per class.}
  \label{fig:policy}
\end{figure}

\textbf{RO4.} The interval a passive observer records becomes a policy value the operator sets,
over a usable range. We installed 16 release policies in turn and ran a fixed DNP3 workload
through each, in two series: the first holding the budget at
$D = D_A + \mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}} = 24$~ms and moving the split between them, the second
fixing the configured $\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$ at 4~ms and raising $D_A$ until the mechanism
leaves its operating region. Each point gives the median over its READ exchanges.

In Figure~\ref{fig:policy}(b) the measured $\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$ follows the configured
value along the identity line: settings of 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20 and 22~ms give measured medians
of 0.998, 1.999, 3.999, 8.001, 12.001, 16.000, 20.001 and 22.001~ms, while the end-to-end response
time stays between 25.30 and 25.34~ms throughout. The leaking interval and the cost of the
exchange can therefore be set independently, which makes the mechanism a framework rather
than one fixed setting. In Figure~\ref{fig:policy}(c) the request-to-acknowledgment interval
tracks $D_A$ up to 30~ms, where the measured median reaches 30.571~ms and
$\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$ still sits at 4.001~ms.

Beyond that the acknowledgment interval saturates at about 31.07~ms and
$\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$ can no longer be held, rising to 5.503, 7.498 and 9.507~ms at $D_A$
of 32, 34 and 36~ms. The reservoir carries a finite pass budget and a hold ends when that
budget is spent, which puts the ceiling close to the admission horizon $H$ the control plane
computes. From below, the outstation's own response time closes the range: in Figure~\ref{fig:policy}(a) the 24~ms budget covers 99.900\% of
Timing OFF read-lane exchanges, leaving one in a thousand arriving too late to be held.
Consequently the usable range becomes 1 to 22~ms at a fixed budget.

\subsection{What does the mechanism cost? (RO5)}
\label{sec:eval:cost}

\textbf{RO5.} In Figure~\ref{fig:policy}(d) the median response time rises by 22.7~ms for READ,
22.6~ms for SELECT and 21.2~ms for OPERATE, each below the 24~ms release budget because the hold
absorbs the outstation's own response time. Each exchange carries 3.017 frames and 272.3 bytes on the
master-facing link, identical in both arms, so the mechanism moves packets in time without adding
a frame or a byte.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{figures/tail/fig_release_tail}
  \caption{The release tail $\varepsilon$ against the budget $D$ for each class, over three
  installs of each setting; whiskers span them, and in the shaded region the reservoir spends its
  pass budget and releases early. OPERATE is timed from the request, so its $\varepsilon$ is the
  acknowledgment's arrival beyond $T_0 + D_A$. Lower right, each configured quantity that could set
  a master-visible OPERATE interval, against the interval it would set: the configured
  $\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$ is obeyed, and the control plane's offsets $A$ and $R-A$ are
  ignored.}
  \label{fig:tail}
\end{figure}

\textbf{The Release Tail.} A held packet leaves after its scheduled instant, and the difference
$\varepsilon$ adds to the hold the operator configured. The switch
timestamps no departure, so we read $\varepsilon$ from the two arms. The Timing OFF
request-to-acknowledgment median carries the outstation's own latency and every propagation term.
The obfuscated median carries the same terms plus the configured hold plus $\varepsilon$, and the
difference leaves $\varepsilon$. On the campaign's captures it is 0.78~ms at the evaluated 24~ms
budget, already contained in the added latency above. In a second sweep on the same testbed we installed
18 read-lane budgets three times each, 27{,}111 exchanges over 96 blocks. For every block the switch
reported back the installed policy and the disabled size carve. In Figure~\ref{fig:tail} $\varepsilon$ does not
follow the budget smoothly. For READ it sits between 18 and 38~$\mu$s at ten budgets and grows
with the hold to 0.96~ms at the other six, and every setting takes the same branch in all three
installs. SELECT shows the same two branches, but at six settings its installs disagree. OPERATE,
timed from the request, carries 0.55 to 0.85~ms at every hold below the knee and no trace of $J$.
The fourth panel confirms~(\ref{eq:control}): the master-visible control intervals follow $D_A$
and the configured $\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$ and ignore the control plane's own offsets.

\textbf{Timeout and Retransmission Headroom.} Holding the acknowledgment consumes the master's
timers, so we measured what it consumed. On the master-facing captures the request bytes were
acknowledged in a separate segment every time, never piggybacked on the response, no request was
sent while earlier bytes were unacknowledged, and neither arm carried a retransmission, a reset or a duplicate acknowledgment.

The longest the master waited for its acknowledgment was 29.2~ms and for its response 77.7~ms.
Withholding every acknowledgment of one request on a separate diagnostic connection, we observed
the master repeat it after 201~ms, about seven times the longest observed wait.

Select validity gives the other timer a held control transaction could violate, since the master
issues its OPERATE only 0.19~ms after the SELECT response reaches it and the mechanism delays that
response: no obfuscated OPERATE exchange was refused for a stale select.

\subsection{Scope and Limitations}
\label{sec:eval:limits}

We evaluate one SEL-751A behind one Tofino-1, in a five-hour campaign of 22 grouped
runs and a second sweep of 96 blocks. We captured only the master-facing link, so the
per-transaction hold $J$ and the relay-facing release at $T_0 + J$ are configured values, and the
release tail $\varepsilon$ is read from the two arms rather than from switch timestamps. The
campaign's manifests record each block's installed policy, and the second sweep also reads it back
from the switch. On the control path the program keeps a released OPERATE's generation until the
next SELECT and drops a retransmission that carries it, so the mechanism provides no exactly-once
delivery: a command lost on the relay-facing link is not repaired by its own retransmission.
