\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

In this section, we evaluate whether the framework changes timing as configured (RO1), what delay
it adds and how that delay interacts with protocol timers (RO3), and what timing information an
adversary still learns (RO2). We first describe the measurements used to answer these questions.

\textbf{Evaluation Cases.} We compare two cases on the testbed of Section~\ref{sec:impl}. In
\emph{Timing OFF}, the switch forwards packets without scheduled holds. In \emph{Obfuscated}, it
applies the request-anchored schedule of Section~\ref{sec:design}. Both cases run the same switch program. To measure what the adversary observes, we timestamp
packets on the master-facing link and compute request-to-ACK time, CLRT from ACK to response, and
total request-to-response time.
These observations do not provide switch-departure or relay-facing timestamps.

\textbf{Datasets and Settings.} We collected 63{,}360 DNP3 exchanges in 132 captures over 5.3 hours, organized into 22 paired runs
with both cases and equal exchange counts in each case. To reduce confounding by drift, we
interleaved Timing OFF and Obfuscated throughout the campaign. The Obfuscated configuration sets
$D_A=20$~ms and CLRT to 8~ms, giving a 28~ms response budget, and installs the command-hold codebook
$\{2,6,12\}$~ms. Readbacks verify each block's configuration, but the per-exchange command hold is
unobserved. Every request in the main campaign received a response, and no exchange was excluded
from the transaction statistics.

To check whether timing follows a change in policy, we also collected 9{,}360 exchanges across
26 sweep settings: 23 release policies and three controls. Each setting has a control-plane readback. We estimate read-lane coverage from the Timing OFF
request-to-response distribution and evaluate realized timing from Obfuscated exchanges, which are
separate observations rather than repetitions of the same relay execution.

We also collected a low-latency fixed-policy study with the seven-ingress-stage image described
in Section~\ref{sec:impl:p4}. This study contains 37{,}500 validated primary exchanges in 125 blocks: 25
policies, five repetitions per policy, and 100 READ, 100 SELECT and 100 OPERATE exchanges per
repetition. Each block also includes two safety status polls outside the primary count. The capture statistics reported zero drops, and an independent packet-filter check found no TCP
retransmissions. The grid uses configured $D_A \in \{5,10,15,20\}$~ms and configured
$\mathrm{CLRT}_{new} \in \{1,2,4,6,8\}$~ms, plus two additional fixed policies at
$(D_A,\mathrm{CLRT}_{new})=(4,4)$~ms and $(12,4)$~ms. It also repeats three reference settings:
Timing OFF at $(20,4)$~ms and Obfuscated at $(20,4)$~ms and $(20,8)$~ms. The grid policies use
$J \in \{0.25,0.5,1\}$~ms for OPERATE; the three references use the historical
$J \in \{2,6,12\}$~ms codebook. Every policy readback contained exactly the three expected
codebook entries.

\subsection{Effectiveness of Timing Replacement}
\label{sec:eval:sec}
\label{sec:eval:ro1}

To evaluate RO1, we ask whether the released interval concentrates around its configured value,
whether the change differs from adding a constant delay, and where the schedule ceases to hold.
We evaluate READ and SELECT together as the read lane, then examine OPERATE separately.

  (a) 1~ms bins, with everything at or above 15~ms in the category on the right. (b) 0.005~ms bins
  within 0.10~ms of the configured value, dashed. This window contains 0.09\% of Timing OFF
  and 99.94\% of Obfuscated transactions, using each case's entire sample as the denominator.}
  \label{fig:hist}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{figures/ndss/fig_replacement_evidence}
  \caption{Replacement versus a constant shift, measured on the master-facing link.
  (a) READ empirical CDFs, including all observations. The shifted curve is a counterfactual
  obtained by adding one constant to Timing OFF samples to match the Obfuscated median.
  (b) Obfuscated-to-Timing OFF sample-variance ratios for each paired run; connecting lines
  join READ and SELECT from the same run. A constant shift preserves variance, giving a
  ratio of one.}
  \label{fig:replacement}
\end{figure}

\textbf{READ and SELECT.} In Figure~\ref{fig:hist}, we show the READ CLRT measured in both cases.
Under Timing OFF, the interval spans 1.007 to 113.480~ms, whereas under Obfuscated, 99.94\% of
READ exchanges lie within 0.10~ms of the configured 8~ms. The READ median moves from 2.121 to 8.005~ms, while its
interquartile range falls from 2.772 to 0.022~ms. SELECT follows the same schedule: its median moves
from 2.041 to 8.005~ms and its interquartile range falls from 2.679 to 0.021~ms.

\textbf{Replacement versus a Constant Shift.} Figure~\ref{fig:replacement}(a) compares the measured
READ distributions with a counterfactual that shifts Timing OFF by one constant to match the
Obfuscated median. The shifted READ distribution retains its standard deviation of 2.889~ms, while the framework
reduces it to 0.989~ms; SELECT falls from 2.267 to 0.017~ms. These reductions agree with the
variable holds in Section~\ref{sec:design:read}: earlier arrivals wait longer, so the change is
not simply the same added delay on every packet.

In Figure~\ref{fig:replacement}(b), we show the READ and SELECT variance ratios for each paired
run, with a line joining the two operations; both ratios remain below one in every run. Under the stronger comparison against every
Timing OFF run, 21 of the 22 Obfuscated READ runs also have lower variance. Because the remaining run contains large deviations from the configured interval, we retain its
observations in the plots, including the long tail discussed below.

\textbf{Control Lane.} For OPERATE, the master-facing CLRT median moves from 2.929 to 8.000~ms,
and the interquartile range falls from 2.729 to 0.006~ms. The measured interval follows the return schedule in~(\ref{eq:control}) under the installed
command-hold codebook. Note that we evaluate timing at the master, since neither the selected $J$
nor the relay-facing release was observed; the result is not a comparison between individual holds.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/ndss/fig_policy_coverage_cost}
  \caption{Release policy and cost. (a) The Timing OFF request-to-response time the budget must

\textbf{Programmable Policy.}
\label{sec:eval:policy}
In Figure~\ref{fig:policy}(b), we vary the configured CLRT from 2 to 26~ms at a fixed response
budget of 28~ms. Measured CLRT medians remain within 6~$\mu$s of the setting, while median
request-to-response time stays between 28.106 and 28.108~ms. Consequently, within these tested
settings, an operator can change the interval without materially changing the total response delay.
This is because changing the split between the two offsets leaves the response deadline fixed,
provided both packets are available before their respective deadlines.

The sweep also shows a holding limit. The measured request-to-ACK median follows a 30~ms deadline
to 30.107~ms, with CLRT still at 3.999~ms for its configured 4~ms. At larger tested deadlines,
ACK timing saturates near 31.07~ms, and CLRT reaches 9.034~ms at a 36~ms setting. Consequently, the sweep identifies where this implementation stops following the schedule under
the tested traffic, while the control-plane horizon remains an estimate rather than a measured
worst-case bound.

\textbf{Coverage and Late Responses.} Figure~\ref{fig:policy}(a) shows the Timing OFF
request-to-response distribution for READ and SELECT. A 28~ms budget covers 99.94\% of these
exchanges; 17 of 29{,}040 exceed it. This is a master-facing estimate of response-budget coverage,
not a direct test of both switch-side availability conditions. In the separate Obfuscated case,
15 of 26{,}400 READ intervals depart from the configured value by more than 1~ms, and the maximum
CLRT is 78.443~ms. The long tail remains visible in the Obfuscated distribution. Because the two cases contain
different exchanges, this comparison cannot attribute individual departures to the 17 Timing OFF
observations.

\subsection{Performance and Release Accuracy}
\label{sec:eval:perf}

To evaluate RO3, we measure the added transaction delay and check whether the observed waits
trigger retransmission or rejection of a control command. We then examine the excess over the
configured ACK deadline to qualify how closely measured timing follows the schedule.

\subsubsection{Transaction Cost}
\label{sec:eval:cost}

\textbf{Bandwidth.} Each capture carries 1{,}448 frames and 130{,}708 bytes on the master-facing
link in both cases, or 3.017 frames and 272.308 bytes per exchange. Consequently, we observe no added external traffic on the captured link, consistent with the
mechanism in Figure~\ref{fig:design}, where blockers remain on internal loopback paths.

\textbf{Latency.} In Figure~\ref{fig:policy}(c), we show request-to-response time in both cases.
The difference between the case medians is 25.426~ms for READ, 25.482~ms for SELECT and 24.652~ms
for OPERATE. These differences between case medians remain below the 28~ms response budget, consistent with
holding responses until the configured deadline; they are not per-packet hold measurements. To assess this cost on our testbed, we check the observed waits against endpoint behavior below;
other deployments must also satisfy their application latency requirements.

\subsubsection{Impact on Protocol Timers}
\label{sec:eval:timers}

\textbf{Retransmission.} Neither case contains a TCP retransmission on the master-facing captures.
Under Obfuscated, the longest observed request-to-ACK and request-to-response waits are 20.19 and
98.55~ms. In a separate test that withheld every acknowledgment of one request, the master
retransmitted after 200.8~ms. The longest campaign ACK wait is about one tenth of that observed timeout. A separate loss trace
recorded a repeated response after 2.969~s; this is one observed repetition interval, not a lower
bound on all relay retransmissions. We use these observations to assess the tested waits, without
treating campaign maxima as bounds on future traffic.

\textbf{Select Validity.} The master issues OPERATE a median of 0.354~ms after receiving its SELECT
response. Because the master waits for that response, its hold consumes part of the select-to-operate
interval, as does the subsequent command hold. All 5{,}280 Obfuscated SELECT and OPERATE exchanges
returned a success status, supporting successful protocol completion at the tested settings.
Physical actuation and relay-facing command multiplicity were not measured.

\subsubsection{Release Accuracy and Blocker Drain}
\label{sec:eval:tail}

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{figures/tail/fig_release_tail}
  \caption{Master-facing excess over the configured ACK deadline, used as a release-tail proxy.
  (a) Against the configured deadline, one series per operation. Past the dashed line, measured
  ACK timing stops following the setting; the excess no longer represents post-deadline release.
  (b) Distribution at the campaign policy, including every value at or above 0.129~ms in the
  rightmost category. Percentages use each operation's full sample. The plotted excess includes
  path and capture effects and does not directly measure blocker-drain time.}
  \label{fig:tail}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Measured Excess.} To examine release accuracy, we subtract configured $D_A$ from the
measured request-to-ACK interval. In Figure~\ref{fig:tail}, we show this master-facing excess, which includes the ACK release tail
together with request transit, return transit and capture effects.
Because we have no switch-departure timestamps, it does not isolate the drain of the blocker queue
or measure the response release tail.

At the campaign setting, the median excess is 0.101~ms for READ and SELECT and 0.107~ms for
OPERATE; the central 99\% of values lie between 36 and 127~$\mu$s. Across configured ACK deadlines
from 4 to 30~ms, the median stays between 0.098 and 0.108~ms. Consequently, the measured excess remains small across the tested settings even as the configured
deadline increases, indicating close tracking of the schedule in this range. Direct blocker-drain
measurements would be needed to separate the contribution of the queues from the other effects. Once release precedes the configured deadline, the same subtraction
no longer measures a post-deadline excess.

\subsection{Residual Timing Information}
\label{sec:eval:ro2}

\textbf{Adversary Models.} To evaluate RO2, we classify READ, SELECT and OPERATE using a random
forest on CLRT alone or on CLRT and request-to-ACK time. Motivated by timing fingerprinting~\cite{formby2016}, we test transaction-class information for one
device by holding out one paired run at a time, with three-class chance balanced accuracy of 0.333.
The fixed adversary trains on Timing OFF and is applied unchanged; the adaptive adversary retrains
on Obfuscated traffic. We also estimate CLRT--class mutual information and compare it with a null
from 1{,}000 within-run label permutations.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{figures/ndss/fig_leakage}
  \caption{Fingerprinting the three operations, one grouped run held out at a time. (a) Balanced
  accuracy by adversary; the fixed adversary is trained on Timing OFF traffic and applied
  unchanged, the adaptive one is retrained on obfuscated traffic. (b) What pooling several
  exchanges of one operation buys the adaptive adversary, from both intervals. Whiskers and bands
  span the 22 runs, which is within-campaign variability and not a confidence interval.}
  \label{fig:leakage}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Fixed Adversary.} In Figure~\ref{fig:leakage}(a), the fixed adversary reaches 0.728
balanced accuracy under Timing OFF using CLRT and 0.803 using both intervals. After applying the classifier unchanged to Obfuscated traffic, we observe accuracy of 0.335 and
0.333, close to chance. CLRT--class mutual information falls from 0.353 to 0.043~bits, but the
remaining estimate exceeds its permutation null
($p=0.001$). Consequently, the failure of this fixed classifier does not imply that the released
timing contains no class information.

\textbf{Adaptive Adversary.} After retraining, accuracy is 0.451 from CLRT alone and 0.445 from
both intervals. The single-exchange classifier using both intervals performs best on OPERATE,
with recall 0.689, compared with 0.411 for READ and 0.235 for SELECT. Figure~\ref{fig:leakage}(b)
examines a separate attack that pools consecutive exchanges of the same operation within a capture.
To construct a signature from each non-overlapping group, we use the mean, standard deviation,
minimum and maximum of each interval. With twenty exchanges, balanced accuracy reaches 0.650 under
Obfuscated, compared with 0.897 under Timing OFF, showing that pooling strengthens the evaluated
adaptive attack.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{figures/ndss/fig_residual_information}
  \caption{Residual timing information in two binary classification tasks on master-facing
  intervals: (a) READ versus SELECT; (b) READ split at each case's median preceding gap,
  measured from the previous response in the same capture. Both use the CLRT

  the first exchange in each capture, whose preceding gap is undefined.}
  \label{fig:residual}
\end{figure}

\textbf{READ versus SELECT.} To examine two operations handled by the same queues, we train and
test a binary classifier separately in each case. Figure~\ref{fig:residual}(a) shows that accuracy
from both intervals falls from 0.785 under Timing OFF to 0.507 under Obfuscated, against chance
of 0.5. The Obfuscated medians are identical: 8.005~ms CLRT and 20.101~ms request-to-ACK time.
The shared schedule is consistent with this reduction in timing differences, although a
near-chance result for one classifier does not establish the absence of all execution-time information.

\textbf{Request Spacing.} Figure~\ref{fig:residual}(b) examines READ alone, split at each case's
median gap from the preceding response to the next request. We omit the first exchange in each
capture, whose preceding gap is undefined. The Obfuscated groups differ by 31~$\mu$s in median
gap, and the classifier separates them at 0.628 accuracy, compared with 0.536 under Timing OFF.
Because the gap defines the groups but is not supplied as a classifier feature, this result
indicates that the released intervals retain information associated with request spacing, even
within one operation.

The traffic pattern also differs across operations: an Obfuscated READ follows its predecessor
by a median of 20.505~ms, whereas OPERATE follows SELECT by 0.354~ms. We expect request spacing and release state to contribute to the adaptive result, but this
observational comparison does not isolate either as the cause. The design argument remains
conditional on the residual effects identified in Section~\ref{sec:design:read}.

\subsection{Lower-Latency Policies}
\label{sec:eval:low-latency}

\textbf{Fixed-Policy Screen.} We first ask how far the 28~ms response budget can be reduced.
Figure~\ref{fig:latency-grid} shows the fixed-policy grid on the seven-ingress-stage image.
At $(D_A,\mathrm{CLRT}_{new})=(5,1)$~ms, the worst-operation median request-to-response time is
6.109~ms, compared with 28.109~ms at the repeated $(20,8)$~ms reference. The corresponding added
medians over Timing OFF are 3.549 and 25.549~ms. The smaller setting has a worst-operation p99 of
15.446~ms, so its configured 6~ms response budget does not bound the observed tail.

We screened random forests, logistic regression and radial-basis-function support-vector machines
using ACK/CLRT and request-spacing features, with pools of 1, 5 and 20 same-operation exchanges.
The fixed attacker trains on Timing OFF; the adaptive attacker trains on Obfuscated traffic.
Tuning uses only training repetitions. No fixed policy meets the declared criterion: a simultaneous
upper bound on balanced accuracy at most chance plus 0.05 for both tasks and all tested attacks.
The $(5,1)$~ms setting reaches 0.493 on the three-class ACK/CLRT task and 0.600 on READ versus
SELECT. Request-spacing attacks reach 1.000 across the grid. These five-repetition results motivate
a longer measurement at low delay; they do not establish a protected operating point.

\textbf{Focused Randomized Run.} We next retain the seven-stage implementation and fix the centers
at $D_A=5$~ms and $\mathrm{CLRT}_{new}=1$~ms. The existing random table selects among four levels
within $\pm0.5$~ms of each center, giving 16 combinations shared by all three operations.
The command-hold codebook remains $\{0.25,0.5,1\}$~ms. We collect 100 interleaved
Obfuscated/Timing OFF pairs, with 100 exchanges per operation in each block: 60{,}000 primary
exchanges and 400 separate safety polls. All requests receive matching responses, capture counters
report zero drops, and the independent TCP audit finds no retransmission flags. Table readbacks
and packet-to-digest joins verify every selected deadline.

Across 10{,}000 Obfuscated exchanges per operation, median request-to-response times are
6.113~ms for READ, 6.116~ms for SELECT and 6.115~ms for OPERATE. The largest added median over
its paired Timing OFF observations is 3.580~ms. The worst-operation p99 is 15.682~ms.
Responses exceeding their selected target by more than 1~ms account for 8.88\%, 8.98\% and
11.98\% of READ, SELECT and OPERATE, respectively. These exchanges complete successfully;
the threshold describes timing error, not the 500~ms transaction timeout. Consequently, the
observed variance includes both the selected randomization and late responses.

\textbf{Held-Out Attackers.} We reserve the first 40 pairs for training and five-fold grouped tuning,
then freeze all models before scoring the later 60 pairs. At pool size 20, the test set contains
300 signatures per class. Figure~\ref{fig:focused-attacks} shows that the fixed ACK/CLRT attacker
reaches at most 0.342 on the three-class task and 0.503 on READ versus SELECT, close to their
chance levels of 0.333 and 0.500. Adaptive training raises these maxima to 0.531 and 0.573.
An additional adaptive attack subtracts the nearest public ACK and CLRT levels before classification;
it reaches 0.566 and 0.575, without using the actual selected offsets as features.

We form approximate simultaneous upper bounds by resampling the 60 test pairs together across
attacks 5{,}000 times and taking the maximum centered deviation. Using 97.5\% per task, the
ACK/CLRT bounds for the original attacks are 0.572 and 0.623, above the respective thresholds
of 0.383 and 0.550. With request spacing, adaptive accuracy reaches 0.988 and 0.987. The immediate
SELECT-to-OPERATE sequence and 400~ms inter-operation spacing expose workload cadence.
Thus, this policy reduces measured latency and disrupts the tested fixed timing attackers, but
it does not meet the full criterion against adaptive timing and cadence-based attacks.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:eval:limits}

\textbf{Devices and Workloads.} We evaluate one SEL-751A, one Tofino-1 and one testbed workload.
The 22 paired runs in the main campaign describe within-campaign variability, and their held-out-run
ranges are not confidence intervals. The low-latency screen uses approximate grouped-bootstrap
confidence bounds from five same-session acquisition repetitions, so it does not provide
independent confirmation. The focused randomized run separates training from 60 later test pairs,
but these remain repetitions on the same device and workload. Multi-device measurements, such as those used
for fingerprinting by Formby et al.\ and Lin et al.~\cite{formby2016, defrec2020}, are needed to
test device-model distinguishability. Because the implementation protects one transaction at a time, requests that cannot be admitted
bypass timing protection, which limits its coverage under concurrent traffic.

\textbf{Residual Information.} The results characterize the evaluated random forests, logistic
regression and radial-basis-function support-vector-machine models and timing features. Adaptive accuracy remains above chance, and packets that miss their release deadlines
can retain arrival-time variation. Neither the near-chance READ/SELECT result nor the observed
association with request spacing establishes that all device information has been removed. In the
low-latency screen, request-spacing features also expose same-session harness cadence, so those
attacks qualify the measurement procedure as well as the timing mechanism.

\textbf{Unobserved Internal Events.} Master-facing captures do not reveal the selected command
hold, relay-facing release, command multiplicity or physical actuation. They also do not directly
measure blocker-drain time. Configuration readbacks establish installed settings; they do not
supply these missing event timestamps or turn the horizon estimate into a measured bound.

\textbf{Retransmitted Commands.} The control lane retains a released OPERATE generation until
the next SELECT and drops a matching retransmission. Consequently, a command lost toward the relay cannot be repaired by its own retransmission, even
though exchanges without that loss completed successfully in the campaign. We leave support for
this recovery to future work.
