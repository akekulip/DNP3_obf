\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

In this section, we perform security and performance evaluation of the framework. The security
evaluation covers RO1 and RO2 (Section~\ref{sec:eval:sec}), and the performance evaluation covers
RO3 (Section~\ref{sec:eval:perf}).

\textbf{Evaluation Cases.} We compare two cases on the testbed of Section~\ref{sec:impl}. In
\emph{Timing OFF}, the switch forwards the acknowledgment and the response of the outstation as they
arrive. In \emph{Obfuscated}, the framework holds and releases them on the schedule of
Section~\ref{sec:design}. Both cases run the same switch program and differ only in the release
schedule. We timestamp every packet on the master-facing link, where the adversary sits, so every
interval we report is one the adversary can measure.

\textbf{Datasets.} We use two datasets. In the main campaign, we collected 63{,}360 DNP3 exchanges in
132 captures and 22 grouped runs, half in each case. We interleaved the two cases over 5.3 hours
so that drift cannot separate them. We set $D_A$ to 20~ms and the configured
$\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$ to 8~ms, which gives a release budget $D$ of 28~ms, and the control
lane draws $J$ from $\{2, 6, 12\}$~ms. A policy sweep installs 26 settings, 23 release policies and
three controls, with 9{,}360 exchanges in total, and a readback from the control plane confirms each
installed setting. Every request in the main campaign was answered, and no exchange was excluded.

\textbf{Adversary Models.} Following Formby et al.~\cite{formbyWhosControlYour2016}, the adversary
classifies each exchange into one of three operations, READ, SELECT and OPERATE, from its CLRT and
its request-to-acknowledgment interval, with a random forest. We report balanced accuracy, for which
chance is 0.333, and hold out one grouped run at a time. We also estimate the mutual information
between the CLRT and the operation against a null built by shuffling the labels within each run
1{,}000 times, which measures the information available to any classifier.

\subsection{Security Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval:sec}

Security evaluation focuses on (i) whether the framework replaces the timing of the outstation with
the configured value (RO1), and (ii) what fixed and adaptive adversaries still learn (RO2).

\subsubsection{Effectiveness of Timing Replacement}
\label{sec:eval:ro1}

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{figures/clrt/fig_clrt}
  \caption{READ CLRT on the master-facing link, as a percentage of each case's own transactions.
  (a) 1~ms bins, with everything at or above 15~ms in the category on the right. (b) 0.005~ms bins
  within 0.10~ms of the configured value, dashed; the percentages name the share of each case that
  falls inside this window.}
  \label{fig:hist}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Effectiveness in RO1.} In Figure~\ref{fig:hist}, we show the READ CLRT the adversary
measures in both cases. Under Timing OFF, the interval spans 1.007 to 113.480~ms and reflects how
long the relay takes to answer. Under the framework, 99.94\% of READ exchanges fall within 0.10~ms
of the configured 8~ms. From the figure, we can see that the medians move from 2.121~ms for READ and
2.041~ms for SELECT to 8.005~ms for both, while the interquartile ranges fall from 2.772 and
2.679~ms to 0.022 and 0.021~ms. A defense that only shifted the interval would keep the Timing OFF
spread, since shifting a distribution leaves its variance unchanged. Shifting the Timing OFF READ
intervals by a constant keeps their standard deviation of 2.889~ms, whereas the framework reduces it
to 0.989~ms for READ and from 2.267 to 0.017~ms for SELECT. The framework therefore replaces the
interval rather than moving it. In 21 of the 22 runs, the variance of the obfuscated READ interval
lies below that of every Timing OFF run, and the remaining run contains responses that arrived after
their deadline.

\textbf{Removing the Execution Time.} READ and SELECT follow the same path through the switch and
differ only in how long the relay takes to answer them. Under Timing OFF, a classifier trained on
the two separates them at 0.785 balanced accuracy from both intervals, against a chance level of
0.5. Under the framework, the same classifier, trained on obfuscated traffic, reaches 0.507, and
the medians of the two classes are identical, 8.005~ms for the CLRT and 20.101~ms for the
request-to-acknowledgment interval. This is because the framework removes the execution time from
both intervals (Section~\ref{sec:design:read}), so nothing remains that separates two operations
that differ only in execution time.

\textbf{Control Lane.} The control lane achieves the same replacement for OPERATE, although the
framework holds the command itself for a varying $J$ before it reaches the relay. The OPERATE median
moves from 2.929~ms under Timing OFF to 8.000~ms, and its interquartile range falls from 2.729 to
0.006~ms. As~(\ref{eq:control}) predicts, $J$ does not appear in the interval.

\begin{figure*}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/ndss/fig_policy_coverage_cost}
  \caption{Release policy and cost. (a) The Timing OFF request-to-response time the budget must
  cover, for the read lane only. (b) Measured against configured interval at a fixed budget
  $D=28$~ms. (c) Request-to-response time per operation, in both cases.}
  \label{fig:policy}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Programmable Policy.} Based on one budget, an operator can place the released interval
anywhere within it. In Figure~\ref{fig:policy}(b), we show that at a fixed budget of 28~ms,
configured values from 2 to 26~ms produce measured medians within 6~$\mu$s of the setting, while the
median request-to-response time stays between 28.106 and 28.108~ms. Consequently, the operator sets
the interval the adversary measures and the delay of the exchange independently. The sweep also
shows the upper end of the range. The acknowledgment follows its deadline up to 30~ms, where the
median request-to-acknowledgment interval is 30.107~ms and the released interval still sits at
3.999~ms, its configured 4~ms. Beyond that setting the switch can no longer sustain the hold, the
acknowledgment leaves at 31.07~ms whatever the setting, and the released interval grows to 9.034~ms
at a 36~ms setting.
\label{sec:eval:policy}

\subsubsection{Effectiveness against Fingerprinting}
\label{sec:eval:ro2}

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{figures/ndss/fig_leakage}
  \caption{Fingerprinting the three operations, one grouped run held out at a time. (a) Balanced
  accuracy by adversary; the fixed adversary is trained on Timing OFF traffic and applied
  unchanged, the adaptive one is retrained on obfuscated traffic. (b) What pooling several
  exchanges of one operation buys the adaptive adversary, from both intervals. Whiskers and bands
  span the 22 runs, which is within-campaign variability and not a confidence interval.}
  \label{fig:leakage}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Effectiveness in RO2.} Under Timing OFF, the CLRT separates the three operations, with
medians of 2.121, 2.041 and 2.929~ms, while their request-to-acknowledgment medians of 0.553, 0.551
and 0.554~ms barely differ. In Figure~\ref{fig:leakage}(a), we show that a fixed adversary trained
on Timing OFF traffic reaches 0.728 balanced accuracy from the CLRT alone and 0.803 from both
intervals. Applied to obfuscated traffic, the same adversary falls to 0.335 and 0.333, i.e.,
chance. The mutual information between the CLRT and the operation falls from 0.353 to 0.043~bits.
The remaining information lies above its permutation null ($p = 0.001$), and the adaptive adversary
below shows where it comes from.

\textbf{Adaptive Adversary.} An adaptive adversary retrained on obfuscated traffic reaches 0.451
from the CLRT alone and 0.445 from both intervals. In Figure~\ref{fig:leakage}(b), we show that
pooling helps it, as it does under Timing OFF. When it pools twenty exchanges of both intervals, its
accuracy rises to 0.650, against 0.897 under Timing OFF. Its accuracy comes almost entirely from OPERATE, which
it recognizes in 0.689 of cases, while it recognizes READ in 0.411 and SELECT in 0.235 of cases. We
find that this accuracy reflects when requests reach the switch rather than how the relay executes
them, for three reasons. First, READ and SELECT, which differ in execution time, are inseparable
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:ro1}). Second, READ exchanges, which share one execution time, become
separable when we split them by the gap that preceded each request. The two halves differ by
31~$\mu$s in their median gap, and the same classifier separates them at 0.628 under the framework,
against 0.536 under Timing OFF. Third, OPERATE reaches the switch in a different way from the other
two operations. The master sends it 0.354~ms after the response to its SELECT, whereas a READ follows
the previous exchange by 20.505~ms, and the release tail of OPERATE has a median of 107~$\mu$s,
against 101~$\mu$s for READ and SELECT. As argued in Section~\ref{sec:design:read}, the arrival of
a request sets the state of the blocker queue it meets, and the master, not the outstation, decides
when each request arrives.

\textbf{Discussion.} Operations of one device differ in execution time, e.g., by 0.8~ms between a
poll and a control command on our relay, and device types differ in the same
way~\cite{formbyWhosControlYour2016, linDefRecEstablishingPhysical2020}. Because the framework
removes the execution time whatever its source (Section~\ref{sec:design:read}), the same argument
applies to device types. Behind the switch, every outstation whose responses arrive within the
budget presents intervals set by the same schedule. What an adaptive adversary still learns lies in
the release logic of the switch and in the schedule of the master, both of which the operator
controls.

\subsection{Performance Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval:perf}

The framework introduces overhead in two forms, bandwidth and latency, and the latency can interact
with the timers of TCP and DNP3. We evaluate each against RO3.

\subsubsection{Bandwidth and Latency Overhead}
\label{sec:eval:cost}

\textbf{Effectiveness in RO3.} We observe no bandwidth overhead. Each of the 132 captures carries
1{,}448 frames and 130{,}708 bytes on the master-facing link in both cases, i.e., 3.017 frames and
272.308 bytes per exchange, so the framework moves packets in time without adding a frame or a
byte. In Figure~\ref{fig:policy}(c), we show the request-to-response time, whose median the
framework raises by 25.426~ms for READ, 25.482~ms for SELECT and 24.652~ms for OPERATE. Each
increase stays below the 28~ms budget, as the latency bound of Section~\ref{sec:design:overhead}
predicts, because the holds absorb the response time of the outstation.

\subsubsection{Release Accuracy}
\label{sec:eval:tail}

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{figures/tail/fig_release_tail}
  \caption{Release tail $\varepsilon$. (a) Against the acknowledgment deadline, one series per
  operation. Past the dashed line the acknowledgment leaves at 31.07~ms whatever the setting, so
  the deadline is not met and no tail is defined. (b) Its distribution at the campaign policy.}
  \label{fig:tail}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Release Tail.} Each held packet leaves after its deadline by the release tail
$\varepsilon$. Because the switch timestamps no departure, we obtain $\varepsilon$ on the
master-facing link, as the request-to-acknowledgment interval minus the configured $D_A$, which
also contains the path between the master and the switch. In the main campaign, the median is
0.101~ms for READ and SELECT and 0.107~ms for OPERATE, and 99\% of all values lie between 36 and
127~$\mu$s. In Figure~\ref{fig:tail}, we show that the tail does not depend on the length of the
hold: across the sweep, its median stays between 0.098 and 0.108~ms for every setting from 4 to
30~ms. Consequently, the release tail stays two orders of magnitude below the timers of
Section~\ref{sec:design:overhead}.

\subsubsection{Impact on Protocol Timers}
\label{sec:eval:timers}

\textbf{Retransmission.} On the master-facing captures, neither case carried a TCP retransmission.
The longest the master waited under the framework was 20.19~ms for an acknowledgment and 98.55~ms
for a response. In a separate test that withheld every acknowledgment of one request, the master
repeated the request after 200.8~ms, about ten times the longest wait for an acknowledgment.

\textbf{Select Validity.} The master issues its OPERATE 0.354~ms after the SELECT response reaches
it, so the hold on that response consumes part of the select-to-operate timeout. No obfuscated
OPERATE was refused, and all 5{,}280 SELECT and OPERATE exchanges returned a success status.

\textbf{Coverage.} In Figure~\ref{fig:policy}(a), we show the Timing OFF request-to-response time of
the read lane against the budget. At 28~ms, the budget covers 99.94\% of exchanges, and 17 of 29{,}040
exceed it. Such a response arrives too late to be held and leaves with its own timing, which gives the
largest released READ interval of 78.443~ms. A larger budget covers more of these exchanges at the
cost of more delay, as argued in Section~\ref{sec:design:overhead}.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:eval:limits}

Four limitations bound these results.

\textbf{For Other Devices.} We evaluate one SEL-751A behind one Tofino-1 switch. Our security
argument in Section~\ref{sec:design:read} does not depend on the device, and the three operations of
the relay, whose execution times differ, exercise it. However, only a measurement across
outstations from several vendors, as Formby et al.\ and Lin et al.\ made for the original
interval~\cite{formbyWhosControlYour2016, linDefRecEstablishingPhysical2020}, can test the
argument for device types directly, and we leave it to future work.

\textbf{For Adaptive Adversaries.} An adaptive adversary learns which operation an exchange carries
from when its request reaches the switch, as shown in Section~\ref{sec:eval:ro2}. This does not give
it the execution time of the relay, and the function code already tells it the operation. However,
an operator who wants the released intervals to carry no arrival information at all needs a release
that does not depend on the state of the blocker queue, which we leave to future work.

\textbf{For the Relay-Facing Link.} We capture the master-facing link, where the adversary sits. As
such, the command hold $J$ and the release toward the relay are configured values rather than
measured ones.

\textbf{For Retransmitted Commands.} After releasing an OPERATE, the control lane keeps its
generation until the next SELECT and drops a retransmission that carries it. A command lost on the
relay-facing link is not repaired by its own retransmission, and extending the control lane to
forward such a retransmission is future work.
