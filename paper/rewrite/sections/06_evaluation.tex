\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{What we measured, and where}
\label{sec:eval:testbed}

\textbf{Evaluation Cases.} We compare two arms of the same program. They differ only in the release
schedule. In \emph{Timing OFF} the switch forwards the outstation's acknowledgment and response as
they arrive; in \emph{Obfuscated} it holds them and releases them on the deadlines of
Section~\ref{sec:design}. The switch is the only path between the master and the relay, and every
timestamp is taken on the master-facing link. Consequently each interval below is one a passive
observer can record: the arrival of the transport acknowledgment to the arrival of the application
response, both on the master's own interface. The switch's internal instants are on links that were not
captured and are recovered from the program rather than measured.

\textbf{Configuration provenance is partial.} Each block's manifest records the policy it was
configured with and the loaded program's identity, but the sweep has no per-point readback of what
the device held. Every policy value in this section is the configured one unless we say otherwise,
and we mark where a readback would have settled a question the configuration cannot.

\textbf{Dataset.} We report per DNP3 exchange. The campaign holds 63{,}360 of them, half with the
mechanism running and half without, collected over five hours in interleaved blocks so that drift
cannot separate the two arms. Every request was answered, and no exchange was retransmitted,
malformed or excluded. A separate sweep on the same testbed covers 16 configured policies and
three controls with the mechanism disabled.

\subsection{Does the released interval reach its configured value? (RO1)}
\label{sec:eval:ro1}

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{figures/clrt/fig_clrt}
  \caption{Measured READ CLRT, at both scales. Columns are the arms, rows the scale. Above,
  common 1~ms bins anchored at 0~ms with a linear ordinate; finite bins are half-open and the
  category past the break holds everything at or above 15~ms, 0.409\% of Timing OFF and 0.027\%
  of Obfuscated. Below, 0.005~ms bins within 0.10~ms of the configured
  $\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$ (dashed), where the mass the upper grid divides between two
  neighbouring bins resolves into one mode. The ordinate is the percentage of that arm's entire
  sample throughout, not of the window, so the rows are comparable; the window holds 13.58\% and
  99.86\%. Above, $n$~=~26{,}400 per arm, mean 2.800 and 4.012~ms, sd 2.609 and 0.628~ms.}
  \label{fig:hist}
\end{figure}

\textbf{RO1.} The read-path schedule of~(\ref{eq:schedule}) does replace
$\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{original}}$ with the configured $\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$. We compare
the two arms over all 22 grouped runs as full empirical distributions, because an adversary reads a single observation against the shape of the
Timing OFF distribution.

Figure~\ref{fig:hist} shows the two READ distributions. Before obfuscation the interval spans
1.01 to 83.46~ms and stays multi-modal, with peaks near 1.2, 2.1 and 4.0~ms; after it, 99.9\% of
transactions fall within 0.10~ms of the configured $\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$. Its lower row
resolves the released interval into a narrow distribution about that value, where the Timing OFF
arm places only 13.6\% of its transactions.

The change is a replacement, not a shift. The standard deviation falls from 2.609 to 0.628~ms for
READ and from 2.267 to 0.024~ms for SELECT. Under a session-level bootstrap those give variance
ratios of 0.058 (95\% CI [0.019, 0.101]) and 0.0001
(95\% CI [1.4$\times$10$^{-5}$, 3.4$\times$10$^{-4}$]). A defense that shifted the interval would keep that spread, since translating a sample leaves its
variance unchanged. Translated onto the protected median, the Timing OFF distribution retains
standard deviations of 2.609 and 2.267~ms, four and ninety-four times what we measure. We computed
that counterfactual analytically, from the Timing OFF samples themselves, and since the loaded
program has no shifting mode, the counterfactual stays analytic and we do not present it as a
measured arm.

Low spread at the wrong interval would not be normalization, so we also report where it sits.
The median $\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{original}}$ is
2.116~ms for READ and 2.050~ms for SELECT, with interquartile ranges of 2.778 and 2.697~ms; under
the mechanism both medians sit at the configured 4.000~ms and each interquartile range falls to
0.006~ms, two orders of magnitude below the 1~ms step between neighbouring policy settings in the
sweep of Section~\ref{sec:eval:policy}. The median alone understates the change, since the Timing OFF distributions rise in a few
discrete steps, allowing one observation to be informative about the class, whereas the obfuscated
ones become a step at the scheduled release, an instant computed from the acknowledgment and a
fixed offset, which no longer depends on how long the device took.
Figure~\ref{fig:hist} shows that for READ; the per-class distributions for all three, and the
per-run medians in acquisition order, are published with the artifact.

A tail remains, and it falls on one side only: the largest obfuscated READ interval reaches
57.182~ms, and fewer
than one READ exchange in a thousand departs from the scheduled release by more than 1~ms. This asymmetry follows from the mechanism, since a response reaching the switch after its
scheduled release cannot be moved backwards and leaves as soon as the hold queue serves it, the
mechanism being unable to schedule a release before the response exists. Below the configured
value the distribution keeps mass as well, since each release carries its own queueing and
service, so their difference falls either side of the configured interval, which gives the
distribution its width. Those late
exchanges are the coverage boundary of RO4 seen from the other side, and
Section~\ref{sec:eval:policy} counts them.

\textbf{Stability within the Campaign.} The obfuscated median sits at the scheduled release in
every one of the 22 grouped runs and every class, while the Timing OFF medians stay separated, so
the effect is not an artifact of one run or of drift over the five hours. The per-run medians and
interquartile ranges in acquisition order are published with the artifact.

\subsection{Does the control lane behave the same way? (RO2)}
\label{sec:eval:ro2}

\textbf{RO2.} The control-path rule of~(\ref{eq:control}) holds the master-visible
response-to-ACK interval at the policy value. Timed from the request, its observable is
$O = \mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$, in which the internal hold $J$ does not appear. Because that
deadline is computed from the request, the read-lane coverage of
Section~\ref{sec:eval:policy} does not describe it, and the switch draws $J$ per transaction from
the configured codebook $\{2, 6, 12\}$~ms. The OPERATE median moves from 2.937~ms under
Timing OFF to 4.000~ms under the mechanism, the interquartile range falls from 2.827 to 0.006~ms,
and the interval stays concentrated at the configured value in every run.

\subsection{What range of settings does the mechanism support? (RO4)}
\label{sec:eval:policy}

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{figures/ndss/fig_policy_coverage_cost}
  \caption{The release policy is programmable and bounded on both sides. (a) Timing OFF read-lane tail
  against the budget $D$ and the horizon $H$. (b) At $D=24$~ms the measured $\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$ follows the
  configured $\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$ while the response latency stays fixed. (c) Raising $D_A$ saturates near $H$.
  (d) End-to-end response time per class.}
  \label{fig:policy}
\end{figure}

\textbf{RO4.} The interval a passive observer records becomes a policy value the operator sets,
over a usable range. We installed 16 release policies in turn and ran a fixed DNP3 workload
through each, in two series: the first holding the budget at
$D = D_A + \mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}} = 24$~ms and moving the split between them, the second
fixing the configured $\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$ at 4~ms and raising $D_A$ until the mechanism
leaves its operating region. Each point gives the median over its READ exchanges.

In Figure~\ref{fig:policy}(b) the measured $\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$ follows the configured
value along the identity line: settings of 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20 and 22~ms give measured medians
of 0.998, 1.999, 3.999, 8.001, 12.001, 16.000, 20.001 and 22.001~ms, while the end-to-end response
time stays between 25.30 and 25.34~ms throughout. The leaking interval and the cost of the
exchange can therefore be set independently, which makes the mechanism a framework rather
than one fixed setting. In Figure~\ref{fig:policy}(c) the request-to-acknowledgment interval
tracks $D_A$ up to 30~ms, where the measured median reaches 30.571~ms and
$\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$ still sits at 4.001~ms.

Beyond that the acknowledgment interval saturates at about 31.07~ms and
$\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$ can no longer be held, rising to 5.503, 7.498 and 9.507~ms at $D_A$
of 32, 34 and 36~ms, because the reservoir carries a finite pass budget and a hold ends when that
budget is spent. This saturation sits close to the admission horizon $H$, as one would expect if the
control plane's assumed service rate holds, yet it remains no evidence that the data
plane enforces $H$. From below, the outstation's own response time closes the range, and
the switch cannot anticipate: in Figure~\ref{fig:policy}(a) the 24~ms budget covers 99.900\% of
Timing OFF read-lane exchanges, leaving one in a thousand arriving too late to be held.
Consequently the usable range becomes 1 to 22~ms at a fixed budget.

\subsection{What does the mechanism cost? (RO5)}
\label{sec:eval:cost}

\textbf{RO5.} In Figure~\ref{fig:policy}(d) the median response time rises by 22.7~ms for READ,
22.6~ms for SELECT and 21.2~ms for OPERATE: close to but below the release budget, because it
depends on how long the outstation itself took, and a real operational cost to weigh against the
polling period and any protocol timeout. Each exchange carries 3.017 frames and 272.3 bytes on the
master-facing link, identical in both arms, so the mechanism moves packets in time without adding
a frame or a byte.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{figures/tail/fig_release_tail}
  \caption{The release tail $\varepsilon$ against the budget $D$ for each class, over three
  installs of each setting; whiskers span them, and in the shaded region the reservoir spends its
  pass budget and releases early. OPERATE is timed from the request, so its $\varepsilon$ is the
  acknowledgment's arrival beyond $T_0 + D_A$. Lower right, each configured quantity that could set
  a master-visible OPERATE interval, against the interval it would set: the configured
  $\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$ is obeyed, and the control plane's offsets $A$ and $R-A$ are
  ignored.}
  \label{fig:tail}
\end{figure}

\textbf{The Release Tail.} A held packet leaves after its scheduled instant, and the difference
$\varepsilon$ is a hold the operator did not configure, so it belongs to the cost. The switch
timestamps no departure, so we read $\varepsilon$ from the two arms: the Timing OFF
request-to-acknowledgment median carries the outstation's own latency and every propagation term,
the obfuscated median carries the same terms plus the configured hold plus $\varepsilon$, and the
difference leaves $\varepsilon$. On the campaign's captures it is 0.78~ms at the evaluated 24~ms
budget, already contained in the added latency above. A second sweep on the same testbed installed
18 read-lane budgets three times each, 27{,}111 exchanges over 96 blocks, reading back the installed policy
and the disabled size carve for every block. In Figure~\ref{fig:tail} $\varepsilon$ does not
follow the budget smoothly. For READ it sits between 18 and 38~$\mu$s at ten budgets and grows
with the hold to 0.96~ms at the other six, and every setting takes the same branch in all three
installs. SELECT shows the same two branches, but at six settings its installs disagree. OPERATE,
timed from the request, carries 0.55 to 0.85~ms at every hold below the knee and no trace of $J$.
The fourth panel confirms~(\ref{eq:control}): the master-visible control intervals follow $D_A$
and the configured $\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$ and ignore the control plane's own offsets.

\textbf{Timeout and Retransmission Headroom.} Holding the acknowledgment consumes the master's
timers, so we measured what it consumed. On the master-facing captures the request bytes were
acknowledged in a separate segment every time, never piggybacked on the response, no request was
sent while earlier bytes were unacknowledged, and neither arm carried a retransmission, a reset or a duplicate acknowledgment.

The longest the master waited for its acknowledgment was 29.2~ms and for its response 77.7~ms.
Withholding every acknowledgment of one request on a separate diagnostic connection, we observed
the master repeat it after 201~ms, about seven times the longest observed wait, consistent with
the absence of a retransmission. This 201~ms remains a measured repetition threshold rather than a reading of the sender's timer,
since the capture records packets rather than socket state, and the next repeat followed 208~ms
later, which simple exponential backoff does not explain. The
receive timeout in our driver sits at 3~s and bounds one read rather than the whole transaction,
so it never acts as a transaction deadline.

Select validity gives the other timer a held control transaction could violate, since the master
issues its OPERATE only 0.19~ms after the SELECT response reaches it and the mechanism delays that
response: no obfuscated OPERATE exchange was refused for a stale select. All of this characterizes
this operating point, and would have to be rechecked for a master with a shorter timeout.

\subsection{What does the attacker still learn? (RO3)}
\label{sec:eval:ro3}

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{figures/ndss/fig_leakage}
  \caption{Transaction-class leakage under the two attacker models, leaving out one grouped run at
  a time. (a) Balanced accuracy by attacker condition and feature set. The abscissa names the
  training condition and the arm it is tested on: fixed on Timing OFF, the same fixed model on
  Obfuscated traffic, and a model retrained on Obfuscated traffic. Whiskers span the 22 held-out
  runs and are not a confidence interval. (b) Mutual information between the CLRT and the class,
  against a within-run permutation null.}
  \label{fig:leakage}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Setup.} The classifier sees the two separately measurable intervals; we exclude their sum,
the total response time, so that no feature forms a linear combination of the others. Separate
measurability does not imply statistical independence and we do not assume it: the classifier
receives both intervals and may exploit any dependence, which the adaptive result below does. We
estimate mutual information for the CLRT in bits against a null built by shuffling class labels
within each grouped run over 1{,}000 permutations, which preserves the per-run class counts.

\textbf{RO3.} Consider first what the mechanism does to the two intervals before any classifier
runs. Under Timing OFF the classes separate mainly along the post-acknowledgment axis, with
medians of 2.116, 2.050 and 2.937~ms for READ, SELECT and OPERATE against
request-to-acknowledgment medians of 0.555, 0.555 and 0.558~ms, which barely separate them at all.
Under the mechanism that axis collapses: all three medians sit at 4.000~ms and READ and SELECT
overlap. What remains is a horizontal offset, with the request-to-acknowledgment median moving to
21.336, 21.239 and 20.650~ms, because the read lane takes its origin from the outstation's
acknowledgment and the control lane from the request. The two intervals plotted against each
other, which shows that collapse and that offset directly, are published with the artifact.

In Figure~\ref{fig:leakage} we show the two attacker models. The fixed adversary trained on
Timing OFF traffic reaches 0.745 balanced accuracy on held-out Timing OFF exchanges using the CLRT
alone, and 0.820 using both intervals, yet applied unchanged to obfuscated traffic it falls to
0.333 in both cases, the chance level. Mutual information between the CLRT and the class falls from
0.383~bits to 0.004~bits, where the Timing OFF value lies far above its permutation null at the
resolution of the test ($p = 0.001$ with 1{,}000 permutations) while the obfuscated value lies
inside its null ($p = 0.084$), so the test does not reject the hypothesis of null noise.
Failing to reject falls short of evidence for zero, since with 1{,}000 permutations the test resolves
differences of this size only so far, and a small residual dependence would look the same.
Consequently, for the evaluated fixed Random-Forest attacker, three-class transaction
identification falls to approximately chance, and no dependence between the cross-layer response
time and the class survives at the resolution of this test. This holds for the two features we give it: the plaintext function code lies outside a
timing-only feature set and still names the operation.

The adaptive adversary gives two results, and they answer different questions. Retrained on
obfuscated traffic it reaches 0.347 on the CLRT alone, against a chance level of 0.333 and with a
per-run range of 0.294 to 0.389 that straddles it: the targeted feature is suppressed against a
retrained adversary, and not against a fixed one alone. The same adversary
recovers to 0.782 given both intervals, since the read and control paths are timed from
different events, leaving their acknowledgment latencies different. Row-normalised confusion over all
22 held-out runs, published with the artifact, shows the shape of that recovery: OPERATE separable
while SELECT stays confused with READ.

We state the cost of that difference rather than leave it to be inferred. On the
request-to-acknowledgment interval alone, balanced accuracy rises from 0.479 under Timing OFF to
0.809 under the mechanism: weakly informative about the class before the mechanism runs, and the
most informative single feature afterwards. The mechanism leaves that feature in place, and
it creates it, because holding the acknowledgment for $D_A$ displaces an interval the two lanes
compute from different events. Against an adversary using both intervals and allowed to retrain,
the net effect falls from 0.820 to 0.782, or 0.038 on a three-class problem with a chance level of
0.333. That margin stays small, and we report it as measured.

Notably the mechanism suppresses the targeted feature under both conditions, the cross-layer response time
alone leaving the fixed adversary at 0.333 and the retrained one at 0.347. What retraining recovers, it
recovers from the request-to-acknowledgment interval, which the mechanism does not target and does
make more informative, and that residual bounds the result. Formby et
al.~\cite{formbyWhosControlYour2016} profile devices from undefended traffic, which motivates the
fixed condition; their setting does not restrict an observer of this mechanism to an unchanged
model, and we do not read it as doing so. Timing both paths from the same event would close the residual, and we leave that to future work.
We have not searched the space of defenses that might close it differently.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:eval:limits}

Each limitation below bounds one of the results above, and we name which.

\textbf{One Device, One Campaign.} The evidence comes from one SEL-751A behind one Tofino-1 in one
five-hour campaign, and since the runs remain grouped collections rather than independent
replications, every result from RO1 to RO5 becomes a statement about this device and this window,
with every spread reflecting within-campaign variability.

\textbf{What RO3 Establishes.} RO3 characterizes the Random-Forest attacker we evaluated rather
than every classifier, and the adaptive adversary recovers 0.782 balanced accuracy from the
difference between the two events, which bounds what it achieves.

\textbf{What RO2 Rests On.} We captured only the master-facing link, so the realized
per-transaction hold $J$ and the relay-facing release at $T_0 + J$ remain configured rather than
observed: RO2 claims only the interval the master sees and reports no result per $J$.

\textbf{What RO4 Observes.} Since the admission horizon $H$ is computed in the control plane
rather than enforced by the data plane, the saturation at 31.07~ms becomes an observed transition
rather than a proof that the two coincide, and the sweep offsets likewise come from the
configuration table rather than a per-point readback.

\textbf{The Residual Is Not Attributed.} The switch's own release instants were not captured, and
the program cannot supply them, since the timestamp registers it declares are never written.
Consequently what the measured distribution shows around the configured value becomes a net
difference between two releases rather than a delay we can place inside the switch.

A separately instrumented build timestamps, in ingress, the last blocker that terminates because
its deadline passed. Over twelve transactions it reports 1{,}706 and 1{,}705~ns on the two lanes,
with standard deviations of 8.7 and 8.0~ns. That interval ends at the last blocking action rather
than at the wire, comes from a different build than the campaign and covers one configuration, so
it bounds neither $\varepsilon$ nor the drain under load. Against the 0.78~ms tail read from the
campaign's captures it is 0.2\%: it measures how soon a blocker sees the expiry, not when the held
packet leaves.

\textbf{What RO5 Counts.} The overhead of RO5 is measured on the master-facing link, where the
switch's internal blocker traffic never appears. The zero-retransmission result is bounded the
same way: because the program suppresses a response retransmission that matches an already-seen
transport position while its transaction is live, such a copy would be absorbed inside the switch
and would not reach a master-facing capture.

We probed that suppression. A response lost downstream of the switch is recovered by exactly such
a retransmission, so a mechanism that discarded it would break loss recovery. Dropping the
response inbound at the master, scoped to one connection, leaves it unacknowledged and the
outstation retransmits on its own timer; three further copies crossed the switch and reached the
master's interface, at 3.20, 9.21 and 20.25~s, every one forwarded rather than absorbed.

The probe does not show that duplicate suppression preserves loss recovery. The filter dropped
every copy for the whole test rather than only the original, so nothing was acknowledged and the
application recovered nothing, and the capture is taken ahead of the filter, so it records arrival
at the interface. The copies also arrived long after the transaction had retired, the first
2.97~s after a transaction live for about 24~ms, so they met the path a stale token takes rather
than the path a duplicate of a live transaction takes, which is the case the concern is about.
Reaching that path needs an outstation whose retransmission timer is of the same order as the
hold; on this relay it is about three orders larger. The mechanism forwards late copies, and the
live-window case stays open.

\textbf{A Lost OPERATE Cannot Be Repaired.} The control lane has a second and worse case, which we
found by reading the program rather than by measuring it. After the switch releases an OPERATE
toward the relay it keeps that command's application generation as a spent marker, and clears it
only when the next SELECT arrives. A later OPERATE carrying the same generation is classified a
duplicate and dropped. If the released command is lost on the relay-facing link, the master's
transport repairs the loss by retransmitting exactly those bytes, with the same generation, and
the switch discards the packet the repair depends on. Forwarding a command out of the switch is
not delivery to the relay, and the program cannot tell the two apart because the relay-facing link
is not instrumented. No capture in this work exercises the path: the campaign's 5{,}280 OPERATE
exchanges lost nothing and every control operation returned success, which is the normal case
working rather than evidence that the failure case is handled. We therefore claim no loss recovery
on the control path, and exactly-once execution is neither provided nor claimed.

\textbf{The Tail Is Not Eliminated.} A fixed read-path release budget leaves about one exchange
in a thousand answering too late to be scheduled, and a comparable fraction of protected READ
exchanges more than 1~ms from the configured value. RO1 is a claim about the distribution, not about every transaction in
it.
