\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

In this section, we perform security and performance evaluation of the framework. The security
evaluation covers RO1 and RO2 (Section~\ref{sec:eval:sec}), and the performance evaluation covers
RO3 (Section~\ref{sec:eval:perf}).

\textbf{Evaluation Cases.} We compare two cases on the testbed of Section~\ref{sec:impl}. In
\emph{Timing OFF}, the switch forwards the acknowledgment and the response of the outstation as they
arrive. In \emph{Obfuscated}, the framework holds and releases them on the schedule of
Section~\ref{sec:design}. Both cases run the same switch program and differ only in the release
schedule. We timestamp every packet on the master-facing link, where the adversary sits, so every
interval we report is one the adversary can measure.

\textbf{Datasets.} We use three datasets. In the main campaign, we collected 63{,}360 DNP3 exchanges
in 22 grouped runs, half in each case, interleaved over five hours so that drift cannot separate the
two cases. We set $D_A$ to 20~ms and the configured $\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$ to 4~ms, and the
control lane draws $J$ from $\{2, 6, 12\}$~ms. A policy sweep installs 16 release policies, and a budget sweep
installs 18 release budgets three times each, 27{,}111 exchanges in total. Every request in the main
campaign was answered, and no exchange was excluded.

\textbf{Adversary Models.} Following Formby et al.~\cite{formbyWhosControlYour2016}, the adversary
classifies each exchange into one of three operations, READ, SELECT and OPERATE, from its CLRT and
its request-to-acknowledgment interval, with a random forest. We report balanced accuracy, for which
chance is 0.333, and hold out one grouped run at a time. We also estimate the mutual information
between the CLRT and the operation against a null built by shuffling the labels within each run
1{,}000 times, which measures the information available to any classifier.

\subsection{Security Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval:sec}

Security evaluation focuses on the effectiveness of (i) the timing replacement (RO1) and (ii) the
resulting resistance to fixed and adaptive fingerprinting (RO2).

\subsubsection{Effectiveness of Timing Replacement}
\label{sec:eval:ro1}

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{figures/clrt/fig_clrt}
  \caption{READ CLRT measured on the master-facing link, in both cases (columns) and at two scales
  (rows). The upper row uses 1~ms bins, and its last bin holds every interval of 15~ms or more. The
  lower row uses 0.005~ms bins within 0.10~ms of the configured $\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$
  (dashed). Both rows give the percentage of all 26{,}400 exchanges of each case.}
  \label{fig:hist}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Effectiveness in RO1.} In Figure~\ref{fig:hist}, we show the READ CLRT the adversary
measures in both cases. Under Timing OFF, the interval spans 1.01 to 83.46~ms, with peaks near 1.2,
2.1 and 4.0~ms that reflect how long the relay takes to answer. Under the framework, 99.9\% of READ
exchanges fall within 0.10~ms of the configured 4~ms. From the figure, we can see that the medians move from 2.116~ms for READ and 2.050~ms for SELECT to
4.000~ms, while the interquartile ranges fall from 2.778 and 2.697~ms to 0.006~ms. The standard
deviation falls from 2.609 to 0.628~ms for READ and from 2.267 to 0.024~ms for SELECT. A defense that only shifted the interval would keep the Timing OFF spread, since shifting a
distribution leaves its variance unchanged. The framework replaces the interval instead of
moving it, and it does so in each of the 22 runs.

\textbf{Control Lane.} The control lane achieves the same replacement for OPERATE, although the
framework holds the command itself for a varying $J$ before it reaches the relay. The OPERATE median
moves from 2.937~ms under Timing OFF to 4.000~ms, and its interquartile range falls from 2.827 to
0.006~ms. As~(\ref{eq:control}) predicts, $J$ does not appear in the interval. In the lower-right
panel of Figure~\ref{fig:tail}, we show that the OPERATE interval follows the configured
$\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$ and ignores every other configured offset.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{figures/ndss/fig_policy_coverage_cost}
  \caption{Release policy and cost. (a) Timing OFF response time of the read lane against the
  budget $D$ and the horizon $H$. (b) At $D=24$~ms, the measured $\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$ against the configured
  one, with the response time of the exchange. (c) The measured request-to-acknowledgment interval
  against the configured $D_A$. (d) End-to-end response time per class in both cases.}
  \label{fig:policy}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Programmable Policy.} Based on one budget, an operator can place the released interval
anywhere within it. In Figure~\ref{fig:policy}(b), we show that at a fixed budget of 24~ms, configured values
from 1 to 22~ms produce measured medians within 2~$\mu$s of the setting, while the end-to-end
response time stays between 25.30 and 25.34~ms. Consequently, the operator sets the interval the
adversary measures and the delay of the exchange independently. In Figure~\ref{fig:policy}(c), we
show the upper end of the range. The acknowledgment hold follows its setting up to 30~ms, where the measured median is
30.571~ms and the released interval still sits at 4.001~ms. Beyond that setting the switch can no
longer sustain the hold, and the released interval rises to 9.507~ms at a 36~ms hold.
\label{sec:eval:policy}

\subsubsection{Effectiveness against Fingerprinting}
\label{sec:eval:ro2}

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{figures/ndss/fig_leakage}
  \caption{Fingerprinting the three operations, with one grouped run held out at a time. (a) Balanced
  accuracy of a fixed adversary tested on Timing OFF and on Obfuscated traffic, and of an adaptive
  adversary retrained on Obfuscated traffic. Whiskers span the 22 held-out runs. (b) Mutual
  information between the CLRT and the operation, against a within-run permutation null.}
  \label{fig:leakage}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Effectiveness in RO2.} Under Timing OFF, the CLRT separates the three operations, with
medians of 2.116, 2.050 and 2.937~ms, while their request-to-acknowledgment medians of 0.555, 0.555
and 0.558~ms barely differ. In Figure~\ref{fig:leakage}(a), we show that a fixed adversary trained
on Timing OFF traffic reaches 0.745 balanced accuracy from the CLRT alone and 0.820 from both
intervals. Applied to obfuscated traffic, the same adversary falls to 0.333 in both cases, i.e.,
chance. In Figure~\ref{fig:leakage}(b), we show the reason. The mutual information between the CLRT
and the operation falls from 0.383~bits, far above its permutation null ($p = 0.001$), to 0.004~bits,
inside it ($p = 0.084$). This is because the framework removes the execution time from the interval
(Section~\ref{sec:design:read}), so the CLRT carries no class information for any classifier to
find.

\textbf{Pooling Adversary.} Formby et al.\ build a signature from many observations rather than
one~\cite{formbyWhosControlYour2016}. We therefore give the adaptive adversary that advantage. The
adversary pools $k$ exchanges of the same operation, summarizes each interval by its mean, spread
and extremes, and retrains on obfuscated traffic. Under Timing OFF, pooling makes the CLRT highly
identifying, and balanced accuracy rises from 0.747 for one exchange to 0.988 for twenty. Under the
framework, the same adversary stays at chance, with 0.347 for one exchange and 0.341 for twenty.
This is because pooling draws from one distribution yields one distribution for every operation,
as argued in Section~\ref{sec:design:read}.

\textbf{Adaptive Adversary.} The adaptive adversary can still read the request-to-acknowledgment
interval, which the framework does not replace. Retrained on both intervals, the adversary reaches
0.782 balanced accuracy from one exchange and 0.942 from five. The source of this accuracy is the
release logic of the switch rather than the execution time of the relay. Because the read lane times
its deadlines from the acknowledgment and the control lane from the request, the adversary
identifies OPERATE in 0.940 of cases. READ and SELECT separate through request-to-acknowledgment
medians of 21.336 and 21.239~ms, a gap that appears only under the framework, since the relay
acknowledges both at a median of 0.555~ms under Timing OFF.

\textbf{Discussion.} Operations of one device differ in execution time, e.g., by 0.8~ms between a
poll and a control command on our relay, and device types differ in the same
way~\cite{formbyWhosControlYour2016, linDefRecEstablishingPhysical2020}. Because the framework
removes the execution time whatever its source (Section~\ref{sec:design:read}), the same argument
applies to device types. Behind the switch, every outstation whose responses arrive within the
budget presents the same released interval. The residual that an adaptive adversary exploits lies
in the release logic of the switch, which the operator configures.

\subsection{Performance Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval:perf}

The framework introduces overhead in two forms, bandwidth and latency, and the latency can interact
with the timers of TCP and DNP3. We evaluate each against RO3.

\subsubsection{Bandwidth and Latency Overhead}
\label{sec:eval:cost}

\textbf{Effectiveness in RO3.} We observe no bandwidth overhead. Each exchange carries 3.017 frames
and 272.308 bytes on the master-facing link in both cases, so the framework moves packets in time
without adding a frame or a byte. In Figure~\ref{fig:policy}(d), we show the end-to-end response
time, which the framework raises by 22.7~ms for READ, 22.6~ms for SELECT and 21.2~ms for OPERATE. Each increase stays below the 24~ms budget, as the latency bound of
Section~\ref{sec:design:overhead} predicts, because the hold absorbs the response time of the
outstation.

\subsubsection{Release Accuracy}
\label{sec:eval:tail}

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{figures/tail/fig_release_tail}
  \caption{Release tail $\varepsilon$ against the budget $D$ for each class, over three installs of
  each setting. Whiskers span the installs. In the shaded region, the switch cannot sustain the
  hold. OPERATE is timed from the request, so its $\varepsilon$ is the arrival of the acknowledgment
  beyond $T_0 + D_A$. Lower right, the master-visible OPERATE interval against each configured
  offset that could set it. Only the configured $\mathrm{CLRT}_{\mathrm{new}}$ does.}
  \label{fig:tail}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Release Tail.} Each held packet leaves after its deadline by the release tail
$\varepsilon$, which adds to the configured hold. Because the switch timestamps no departure, we obtain
$\varepsilon$ from the two cases, as the obfuscated request-to-acknowledgment median minus the
Timing OFF median minus the configured hold. In the main campaign, $\varepsilon$ is 0.78~ms at the
24~ms budget, already included in the latency above. In Figure~\ref{fig:tail}, we show $\varepsilon$
across the budget sweep. For READ, it stays between 18 and 38~$\mu$s at ten budgets and reaches
0.96~ms at the other six, and for OPERATE it stays between 0.55 and 0.85~ms. For every class and
every budget the switch can sustain, the median release tail stays below 1~ms, two orders of
magnitude below the timers of Section~\ref{sec:design:overhead}.

\subsubsection{Impact on Protocol Timers}
\label{sec:eval:timers}

\textbf{Retransmission.} On the master-facing captures, neither case carried a TCP retransmission,
a reset or a duplicate acknowledgment. The longest the master waited under the framework was
29.2~ms for an acknowledgment and 77.7~ms for a response. In a separate test that withheld every
acknowledgment of one request, the master repeated the request after 201~ms, about seven times the
longest wait.

\textbf{Select Validity.} The master issues its OPERATE 0.19~ms after the SELECT response reaches
it, so the hold on that response consumes part of the select-to-operate timeout. No obfuscated
OPERATE was refused, and all 5{,}280 SELECT and OPERATE exchanges returned a success status.

\textbf{Coverage.} In Figure~\ref{fig:policy}(a), we show the Timing OFF response time of the read
lane against the budget. At 24~ms, the budget covers 99.900\% of exchanges. The remaining one in a
thousand arrives too late to be held and leaves with its own timing, which gives the largest
released READ interval of 57.182~ms. A
larger budget covers more of these exchanges at the cost of more delay, as argued in
Section~\ref{sec:design:overhead}.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:eval:limits}

Three limitations bound these results.

\textbf{Single Device.} We evaluate one SEL-751A behind one Tofino-1 switch. Our security argument
in Section~\ref{sec:design:read} does not depend on the device, and the three operations of the
relay, whose execution times differ, exercise it. Measuring the released interval across
outstations from several vendors, as Formby et al.\ and Lin et al.\ did for the original
interval~\cite{formbyWhosControlYour2016, linDefRecEstablishingPhysical2020}, would test the
argument for device types directly, and we leave it to future work.

\textbf{Unobserved Relay-Facing Link.} We capture the master-facing link, where the adversary sits.
As such, the command hold $J$ and the release toward the relay are configured values rather than
measured ones.

\textbf{Retransmitted Commands.} After releasing an OPERATE, the control lane keeps its generation
until the next SELECT and drops a retransmission that carries it. A command lost on the relay-facing
link is not repaired by its own retransmission, and extending the control lane to forward
such a retransmission is future work.
