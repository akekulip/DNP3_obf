# Review — "Programmable In-Network Timing Obfuscation for DNP3"

**Venue:** NDSS 2027 · **Reviewer expertise:** X (ICS/CPS security, programmable data planes, traffic analysis)

---

## 1. Summary of the paper

The paper targets cross-layer response time (CLRT) fingerprinting of DNP3 outstations — the interval between an outstation's TCP acknowledgment and its application response, which Formby et al. (NDSS '16) showed identifies device model and operation. The proposed defense is a P4 program on an Intel Tofino-1 placed between the DNP3 master and the outstation. For READ and the SELECT phase of select-before-operate, the switch arms two release deadlines from a single instant $t_A$ (the arrival of the outstation's ACK), so that $\mathrm{CLRT_{original}}$ algebraically cancels and the master-visible interval equals a configured constant $\mathrm{CLRT_{new}}$. For OPERATE, deadlines are anchored to the request $T_0$ instead, and the OPERATE itself is held for an internal delay $J$ toward the relay. Holds are implemented without a timer, by parking each held packet beneath a strict-priority queue fed by recirculating "blocker" packets. Evaluation is on one physical SEL-751A relay, 63,360 exchanges in 22 grouped runs, plus two policy/budget sweeps.

The contribution is crisply stateable, which is a good sign: *replace, rather than perturb, one cross-layer timing observable, in the network, with no added frames or bytes, on unencrypted ICS traffic.* The delta over Ditto/Minos/Securitas (size-and-pattern reshaping with cover traffic) and over DefRec/RAINCOAT (semantic decoys) is real and well positioned.

That said, I cannot recommend acceptance in the current form. Three problems are not presentational: the abstract and RO2 assert a result that the paper's own Section VI-A2 contradicts; the security argument in Section IV-B contains an internal contradiction about the release tail $\varepsilon$; and the single most adverse measurement in the authors' own artifact — that the framework *increases* single-feature leakage on the request-to-ACK interval from 0.479 to 0.809 balanced accuracy — does not appear anywhere in the manuscript.

---

## 2. Strengths

**S1. Real hardware, real device, honest tail reporting.** A physical SEL-751A behind a physical Tofino-1, 63,360 exchanges, maxima reported rather than clipped (57.182 ms for the obfuscated READ tail, §VI-B3), and the coverage residual quantified at 0.0999%. This is substantially more than most in-network defense papers offer, and the release-tail measurement (Fig. 8) is the kind of self-scrutiny reviewers rarely see.

**S2. The mechanism is genuinely clever and correctly motivated.** The "Constant Holds" argument in §IV-B (p. 5) — that constant delay only translates the distribution and leaves the variance, hence the fingerprint, intact — is the right observation, and the dual-deadline construction is the right response to it. Implementing an untimed hold with a strict-priority blocker queue and recirculation is a real data-plane contribution, not a simulation.

**S3. The zero-byte property is a genuine differentiator for unencrypted ICS.** §IV-D's bandwidth argument is sound and the measurement backs it (3.017 frames / 272.308 bytes per exchange in both arms). Against a plaintext protocol where injected chaff is trivially identifiable, this is the correct design point, and the related-work paragraph on Pacer/NetShaper correctly identifies why host-side shaping does not apply.

**S4. Statistical hygiene in places where it would have been easy to cheat.** The MI permutation null, the leave-one-grouped-run-out protocol, and — per the project's own record — the withdrawal of two indefensible uncertainty estimates (a jackknife CI on a near-boundary kNN MI estimator, and a bootstrap over training-coupled folds) are commendable. Very few submissions withdraw a published interval.

## 3. Findings

### CRITICAL (blocks acceptance)

---

**C1. [CLAIMS] The abstract and RO2 assert that the adaptive pooling adversary stays at chance; Section VI-A2 shows it reaching 0.942.**

Abstract, p. 1 (`00_abstract.tex:18-19`):

> "A classifier trained before deployment falls from 0.745 balanced accuracy to three-class chance, **an adaptive adversary that pools many exchanges stays at chance**, and the framework adds no frame and no byte."

Conclusion, p. 10 (`08_conclusion.tex:13-15`) and Intro bullet, p. 2 (`01_introduction.tex:104-105`) repeat it. RO2, p. 3 (`03_threat_model.tex:52-54`), states the objective as:

> "**for fixed and adaptive adversaries**, we aim at removing the information the CLRT carries about the outstation, such that **their classifiers perform no better than chance, even when they pool many exchanges**."

Section VI-A2, p. 8 (`06_evaluation.tex:112-114`), then reports:

> "Retrained on both intervals, the adversary reaches 0.782 balanced accuracy from one exchange and **0.942 from five**."

0.942 is `pooled_adversary.balanced_accuracy["ack_clrt/obfuscated/k5"] = 0.9424` in `MANUSCRIPT_VALUES.json:206` — i.e. it *is* a pooled, adaptive result. The abstract's "pools many exchanges stays at chance" is true only for the CLRT-only feature set, a restriction the abstract does not state and RO2 explicitly disclaims ("for fixed and adaptive adversaries"). The section is nonetheless headed "**Effectiveness in RO2**" and nowhere says RO2 is met only partially. `CLAIMS_AND_LIMITATIONS.md` C6 is explicit that the adaptive attacker recovers; the manuscript's abstract exceeds its own boundary document.

*Fix:* Restate the abstract and the RO2 bullet to scope the chance-level result to the replaced interval, and add one sentence to §VI-A2 that says in plain words that RO2 is achieved for the CLRT and not for the adversary as specified. A reviewer who reads only the abstract and then Table/Fig. 7 will conclude the paper is overselling; do not let that happen.

**C2. [CLAIMS/EVAL] The framework makes one observable substantially *more* identifying than before deployment, and that number is absent from the paper.**

`MANUSCRIPT_VALUES.json:147-150`:

```
"req_to_ack_only": { "native": 0.4794, "protected": 0.8092 }
```

The request-to-ACK interval alone yields 0.479 balanced accuracy under Timing OFF and **0.809 under the framework**. Section VI-A2 gestures at the mechanism —

> "READ and SELECT separate through request-to-acknowledgment medians of 21.336 and 21.239 ms, **a gap that appears only under the framework**, since the relay acknowledges both at a median of 0.555 ms under Timing OFF."

— but never states the headline consequence: the defense converts a near-useless feature (0.479, barely above 0.333 chance) into the strongest single feature in the dataset (0.809). That is a defense that *creates* a side channel. Omitting the number while describing the mechanism qualitatively reads, to a hostile reviewer, as selective reporting of an adverse result. It is also the number that most sharply bounds the contribution.

*Fix:* Report 0.479 → 0.809 explicitly in §VI-A2, add the req-to-ACK-only condition as a bar group in Fig. 7(a), and confront it in the Discussion: the release logic is a configurable, operator-set quantity, so the paper can argue the leak is removable (e.g. by anchoring both lanes identically, or by randomizing $D_A$ per transaction) — but it must argue it, not omit it.

**C3. [CLAIMS] The Section IV-B security argument contradicts itself on $\varepsilon$, and the contradiction is load-bearing.**

Page 5, two paragraphs apart in the *same* subsection. First:

> "$e_R - e_A = \mathrm{CLRT_{new}} + \varepsilon_R - \varepsilon_A$ (8). **No term on the right-hand side depends on $t_R$.** Consequently, the execution time of the outstation does not reach the interval the adversary measures."

Then, twelve lines later:

> "The device can still reach the interval in only two ways, **through release tails that depend on the length of the hold** and through responses that violate (7)."

By the paper's own Eq. (6), the response hold is $e_R - t_R = D_A + \mathrm{CLRT_{new}} - \mathrm{CLRT_{original}}$ — a function of $t_R$. So if $\varepsilon_R$ depends on the length of the hold, $\varepsilon_R$ depends on $t_R$, and the sentence "no term on the right-hand side depends on $t_R$" is false as written. This is not pedantry: Fig. 8(a) shows $\varepsilon$ for READ ranging from ~18 µs to ~960 µs, i.e. **two orders of magnitude larger than the reported obfuscated IQR of 0.006 ms (6 µs)**. Any within-arm dependence of $\varepsilon$ on $\mathrm{CLRT_{original}}$ would therefore dominate the residual channel the paper claims to have closed.

Compounding this, §IV-B ends with "We evaluate both in Section VI" — and Section VI evaluates *neither* leak channel as a leak. §VI-B2 measures the median $\varepsilon$ per budget (a performance measurement, in the performance section) but never tests whether $\varepsilon$ correlates with $\mathrm{CLRT_{original}}$ within an arm. §VI-B3 reports the late fraction (0.0999%) and the largest late interval but never trains a classifier on the late subset — yet those ~29 exchanges per 29,040 carry the device's *unobfuscated* CLRT exactly, are trivially identifiable (CLRT $\neq$ 4.000 ms), and accumulate over hours of polling into a clean sample of the original distribution. The design section promises an evaluation that the evaluation section does not deliver.

*Fix:* (i) Rewrite Eq. (8)'s conclusion as a conditional — the interval is independent of $t_R$ *to the extent that $\varepsilon_R - \varepsilon_A$ is*. (ii) Add the missing experiment: regress or mutual-information $\varepsilon$ against $\mathrm{CLRT_{original}}$ per transaction within the obfuscated arm. (iii) Add a "late-exchange adversary" that filters obfuscated traffic for CLRT outside the configured value and classifies on that subset. Both are re-analyses of existing captures; neither needs new hardware runs.

**C4. [THREAT/EVAL] The switch protects one transaction at a time and three function codes; everything else fails open and carries the fingerprint, and none of this is evaluated.**

§V-B, p. 7 (`05_implementation.tex:56-59`):

> "Both lanes share one set of deadline registers, so the program **protects one transaction at a time. A second transaction that would arm while one is in flight is forwarded unprotected** rather than disturbing the deadline in progress."

§II-A, p. 2: "We limit the framework to the three function codes above, READ, SELECT and OPERATE, and **it forwards every other exchange unchanged**."

The evaluation uses one master, one outstation, serialized polls — a workload constructed so that concurrency never occurs. The paper never reports how many exchanges were forwarded unprotected (I would expect "zero", which is precisely the problem: it is zero *by construction of the workload*). In any realistic substation the switch sits in front of multiple IEDs, a master runs integrity and event polls concurrently, and DNP3 outstations emit **unsolicited responses**, which this design does not touch at all. A passive adversary needs only one unprotected exchange class to recover the device's CLRT distribution; the paper's own threat model grants that adversary unlimited passive observation time.

Relatedly, DNP3 function code 23 is *Delay Measurement* — an explicitly timing-bearing exchange that the framework forwards unchanged.

*Fix:* This is the largest gap between what the paper claims ("disrupt timing-based fingerprinting") and what it demonstrates (three function codes, one device, no concurrency). At minimum: (a) state the single-in-flight-transaction limitation in §IV, not only in §V; (b) report the unprotected-exchange fraction in the campaign; (c) run one multi-session or interleaved-poll workload, or, if the hard rule against further experimentation stands, scope RO1/RO2 explicitly to "one protected session with serialized transactions" throughout, including in the abstract; (d) discuss unsolicited responses and the untouched function codes in §III-B with the reconnaissance consequence spelled out.

---

**C5. [EVAL] No defense baseline is measured. The only alternative defense is dismissed by a statistical truism.**

§VI-A1, p. 8:

> "**A defense that only shifted the interval would keep the Timing OFF spread**, since shifting a distribution leaves its variance unchanged."

This is an identity, not an experiment. `MANUSCRIPT_VALUES.json:129` contains `counterfactual_shifted_native_sd = 2.609171` — numerically identical to `native_sd`, confirming it is an analytic counterfactual, and the paper does not even report it. There is no measured comparison against (i) a constant-shift defense, (ii) per-response random jitter drawn from a wide distribution (the obvious, cheap, deployable alternative that every reviewer will name), or (iii) a host-side sleep in the outstation's DNP3 stack. Without at least one of these run through the *same* classifier pipeline, the reader cannot tell how much of the 0.745 → 0.333 drop is attributable to the dual-deadline construction rather than to any large delay.

*Fix:* Random jitter is the baseline that matters. Run the identical Random-Forest pipeline on a synthetic arm constructed from the Timing OFF captures with $\mathrm{CLRT}$ replaced by $\mathrm{CLRT_{original}} + U$ for a jitter $U$ matched in mean delay to the framework's 22.7 ms, and report its balanced accuracy and MI. This is a pure re-analysis of existing captures and it is the single cheapest thing you can do to raise the score.

**M4. [FIGURES] Fig. 3 contradicts the implementation text on queue count, and puts the outstation on both sides of the switch.**

`figures/fig_design.png`, printed as Fig. 3 on p. 4. The read lane is drawn as **three** rows — one orange blocker row above an ACK row and a response row. §V-A, p. 6, states:

> "the read lane uses **four queues** on one internal port. The figure draws one shared blocker queue; the text insists on two, and the independence of the two releases — which the whole dual-deadline construction requires — depends on there being two. The figure undercuts the mechanism.

*Fix:* Draw four queues in the read lane. Either make Fig. 3 physically consistent with Fig. 2, or mark it unambiguously as a logical view. Add a priority annotation to the blocker rows.

**M8. [CLAIMS] The obfuscated standard deviation (0.628 ms) is 100× the headline IQR (0.006 ms), and the paper never says why.**

§VI-A1, p. 8: "The standard deviation falls from 2.609 to **0.628 ms** for READ and from 2.267 to **0.024 ms** for SELECT." Meanwhile the abstract, introduction and conclusion all lead with the IQR collapse to 0.006 ms. The READ SD of 0.628 ms is dominated by the ~29 late exchanges with a maximum of 57.182 ms. Quoting the IQR three times in the paper's most prominent positions while burying the SD — the dispersion measure that an adversary with enough samples actually exploits — will be read as choosing the flattering statistic. Note the SELECT SD falls to 0.024 ms (26× smaller than READ's) purely because SELECT has fewer late exchanges; the gap between the two is itself informative and unexplained.

*Fix:* One sentence in §VI-A1 attributing the residual SD to the coverage tail, with the count. This turns an apparent evasion into a demonstration of rigor.

**M9. [CLAIMS] "No class information for any classifier to find" overclaims the MI result and breaches L8.**

§VI-A2, p. 8:

> "the CLRT carries **no class information for any classifier** to find."

MI = 0.004 bits with an empirical $p = 0.084$ means the estimate is not separable from the null *at this sample size with this estimator*, not that the information is zero. The paper's own limitation record scopes the result to the evaluated Random-Forest attacker and places no interval on the MI point estimate. "Any classifier" is exactly the universal quantifier the limitation forbids.

The same pattern appears in §IV-B: "makes it **challenging, if not impossible**, to perform better than chance" — "impossible" is unsupported, and the sentence is also grammatically unmoored (the subject of "to perform" is the classifier, three clauses away).

Also note: the paper never names the mutual-information estimator. Per the project record it is a nearest-neighbour estimator, which near the zero-information boundary is bounded, biased and non-smooth — a fact that matters enough that two uncertainty estimates were withdrawn over it. The manuscript must name the estimator and cite it (Kraskov–Stögbauer–Grassberger, or whichever was used).

**M10. [CLAIMS] The generalization from three operations to device types is stated as a finding, not an argument, and it crosses the stated boundary.**

§VI-A2 Discussion, p. 9:

> "Because the framework removes the execution time whatever its source (Section IV-B), the same argument applies to device types. **Behind the switch, every outstation whose responses arrive within the budget presents the same released interval.**"

The second sentence is written as a result. It is false in the three ways the paper's own data identifies: (i) a slower device has a larger late fraction, and the late fraction is itself a per-device signature; (ii) the request-to-ACK interval is a per-device TCP-stack property that the framework does not replace and, per C2, amplifies; (iii) $\varepsilon$ depends on hold length and therefore on the device's response distribution. The Limitations paragraph ("Single Device", p. 9) is honestly written, but it does not retract the Discussion's stronger sentence.

Further, the **abstract** frames the fingerprint as identifying "both the device and the operation it performs", and the framework as disrupting "timing-based fingerprinting" — while the evaluation is a three-class *transaction-class* problem on a single device. Your own claim boundaries say this is "not device fingerprinting and not a device-model separation result." An NDSS reviewer will mark this as an abstract that promises device fingerprinting resistance and an evaluation that delivers operation-class resistance.

*Fix:* Reword the Discussion sentence as a conjecture with named residuals. Make the abstract say what was measured: the interval no longer separates the *operations* of one device. The device-type argument is a fine thing to state as an argument and a fine reason to ask for future work; it is not a fine thing to state as an observation.

**M11. [CLAIMS] The firstness claim is present despite the project's own gate forbidding it, and it is not established by the related work.**

§I contributions, p. 2 (`01_introduction.tex:78-80`):

> "**To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work that replaces the cross-layer response time of an ICS device inside the network.** Existing defenses mimic, randomize or encrypt what the device sends."

Two problems. First, the conjunction is narrow enough ("cross-layer response time" ∧ "ICS device" ∧ "inside the network") that it reads as manufactured firstness — reviewers dislike claims whose scope was chosen to make them true. Second, it is not supported by §VII, which nowhere establishes that no prior work replaces a response-time interval in-network; the related work surveys size/pattern reshaping and semantic decoys and then asserts the gap. Note also that Formby's own patent family (US 11,228,517 / WO2016144793) discusses CLRT and countermeasures, and CLRT forgery via timed sleeps has been discussed in the follow-on literature. Host-side CLRT forgery does not defeat your "inside the network" clause, but it does defeat "Existing defenses mimic, randomize or encrypt what the device sends" if it is not cited.

*Fix:* The paper is strong enough without the claim. Delete it, or replace it with a positioning sentence ("We are not aware of prior work that …") and add a paragraph to §VII acknowledging host-side CLRT forgery.

**M13. [REFS] Missing related work a reviewer will name.**

I would demand, at minimum:

1. **Modern website-fingerprinting defenses (2022–). The related work stops at WTF-PAD (2016) and Tamaraw (2014).** For an NDSS 2027 submission this is an eight-year gap on the closest methodological neighbours. Name and position: **Surakav** (Gong et al., IEEE S&P 2022, GAN-generated shaping traces), **RegulaTor** (Holland & Hopper, PoPETs 2022), **QCSD** (Smith et al., USENIX Security 2022 — client-side, no server change, directly analogous to your "no endpoint modification" framing), and **Maybenot** (Pulls, a defense framework). Your §VII sentence "the attacks that follow them have become correspondingly strong" cites only 2016–2018 attacks.

2. **In-network ICS security on programmable data planes.** "Leveraging Data Plane Programmability Towards a Policy-driven In-Network Security Framework for Industrial Control Systems" (ACM e-Energy 2025) — Modbus filtering and mitigation on Tofino. This is the closest in-network-ICS system to yours and its absence is conspicuous next to your citation of Hu et al. [39].

3. **Deterministic in-network scheduling.** "Programmable Cycle-Specified Queue for Long-Distance Industrial Deterministic Packet Scheduling" (arXiv 2409.09592) — per-packet deadline scheduling on programmable hardware for industrial traffic. This is direct mechanism prior art for "hold a packet until an absolute instant in a switch" and you should distinguish your blocker-queue construction from it.

4. **Recent ICS timing fingerprinting.** "TIDF: Timing-Based Device Fingerprinting for PLCs" (Springer, 2025) and "Time Constant: Actuator Fingerprinting using Transient Response of Device and Process in ICS" (arXiv 2409.16536). Both post-date everything in your §VII "ICS and Power-System Fingerprinting" paragraph except Pospisil 2023, and both bear on whether replacing one interval is sufficient.

5. **Scheduling-based privacy.** Beams et al., "Packet Scheduling with Optional Client Privacy" (CCS 2021) — scheduling as the privacy primitive, which is exactly your primitive.

6. **An operational-latency standard.** **IEEE Std 1646-2004**, "Communication Delivery Time Performance Requirements for Electric Power Substation Automation." Your latency argument (§IV-D, §VI-B1) is made *only* against protocol retransmission timers. A power-systems reviewer will immediately ask whether +22.7 ms per exchange is acceptable against substation automation delivery-time classes, which are considerably tighter than a TCP RTO. "No packet was retransmitted" is not the same as "the control loop still meets its requirement."

**Reference misuse (in the advisor-supplied paragraph 3; see §5 below).** Refs [15], [16], [17] are cited collectively for "uses the latency between the TCP acknowledge packets and the actual response from the same device to estimate execution time there." Ahmed et al., "Scanning the Cycle" [16], fingerprints PLC **scan-cycle** timing, not TCP-ACK-to-response cross-layer latency; Pospisil et al. [17] uses **dataflow** time delay. Neither supports the specific claim as stated. This needs correcting regardless of the verbatim-installation contract, because it is a factual misattribution to third parties.

**M16. [THREAT] The threat model's central assumption is asserted with a hedge and never revisited.**

§III-A, p. 3:

> "We assume that the adversary … **cannot** read the internal state of the switch or monitor the link between the switch and the outstation. This is a practical assumption, as the switch sits at the edge of the outstation, and the relay-facing link **can be** a single cable between two devices in the same substation cabinet."

The whole defense is void if the relay-facing link is observable, and "can be a single cable" is a statement about a favourable case, not about deployments. Substations routinely carry test switches, span/mirror ports, and protection-engineering taps. The paper should say plainly: if the adversary can tap the relay-facing link, the framework provides no protection, and here is why the deployment assumption is nonetheless reasonable. State it as an assumption with a consequence, not as a reassurance.

Related: the adaptive adversary is strong in one respect (knows the framework, knows $\mathrm{CLRT_{new}}$, relabels with plaintext function codes, pools) and weak in three: it is restricted to two hand-chosen features, one classifier family, and never uses the release tail or the late-exchange subset (see C3). An NDSS reviewer will call the adaptive adversary under-powered.

## 6. Verdict

**Major Revision** (as an NDSS score: **2 — weak reject** in the current cycle, with genuine encouragement to resubmit).

The mechanism is novel, the hardware work is real, and the measurement discipline in the underlying artifact is better than most submissions. What blocks acceptance is not the science — it is that the paper's front matter claims a result its own evaluation section refutes (C1), that the central security argument contradicts itself in the space of twelve lines (C3), and that the most adverse number in the artifact is not in the paper (C2). Reviewer 2 will find all three, and finding them will cost you the benefit of the doubt on everything else.

**The three changes that would most raise the score, in order:**

1. **Make the abstract, RO2 and the conclusion say what was measured.** Scope the chance-level claim to the CLRT feature, report req-to-ACK-only 0.479 → 0.809, and state explicitly that the adaptive adversary pooling five exchanges reaches 0.942. A paper that owns its residual is far stronger than one that has to be caught. This is a one-day edit and it moves the paper from "overclaims" to "honest and bounded."

2. **Repair Section IV-B and honour its forward promise.** Restate Eq. (8)'s conclusion as conditional on $\varepsilon_R - \varepsilon_A$, then actually run the two experiments the section says it will run — $\varepsilon$ versus $\mathrm{CLRT_{original}}$ within-arm, and a classifier on the late-exchange subset. Both are re-analyses of existing captures. If both come back null, you have converted the paper's weakest argument into its strongest.

3. **Add a defense baseline and bound the deployment scope.** Run the random-jitter counterfactual through the identical pipeline (re-analysis, no hardware needed), and state the single-in-flight-transaction and three-function-code limitations in Section IV rather than burying them in Section V and Section VI-C. Then fix Fig. 3's queue count, Fig. 5's caption, Fig. 6(a)'s axis/text mismatch, and Fig. 8(d)'s unnamed series.

Everything in M12–M13 (references) and the Minor list is a week of careful work and should be done in the same pass.
