Reconnaissance side-channel · read-only observer

You can identify a grid device from the length of its pauses — even when you can't read a word it says.

A DNP3 outstation (a sensor/relay box in a substation) answers its master over an unencrypted link. An eavesdropper who only watches the wire can't be stopped from reading the bytes — but the bytes aren't the only leak. How long the device takes to answer is a fingerprint of what it is.

This page rebuilds the idea from scratch, one interactive panel at a time, so you can form your own intuition and invent your own moves. Everything is playable — drag, toggle, and watch what the eavesdropper learns. Start below.

The observer never sees inside the device. It only times the gap between the request it saw leave and the answer it saw return. That gap is the processing time — and the processing time is the tell.

Observe · the threat model

The whole attack is: watch, and time.

Picture a substation LAN. A master (control center) polls an outstation (the field device) for readings. On most DNP3 links there's no encryption and no authentication, so anyone with a tap or a mirrored switch port can read the traffic. Our adversary is passive: it never injects, blocks, or alters a packet. It just observes.

That sounds weak. It isn't. Even without decoding a payload, the observer sees the size of each response, how it's chopped into frames, and the timing of everything. Those three together fingerprint the device — its type, its configuration, roughly how many sensors it has — and the fingerprint is stable across sessions because the device behaves deterministically.

size
how many bytes come back — bigger database, bigger answer
shape
how the answer is split into frames on the wire
timing
how long the device pauses before, and between, its replies

The project already has a defense for size and shape (a byte-preserving "splitting" trick). This page is about the third axis — timing — which the paper's title promises but hasn't yet delivered. Timing is the one axis you can reshape without changing a single byte, which is exactly why it's the natural next move.

Analogy: you're in a room where you can hear that someone answered a question and how long they hesitated, but not what they said. A instant reply, a two-second pause, a long deliberation — each hints at who is answering. Timing is that hesitation.

Measure · what the clock actually reads

The gap you time is the device "thinking."

When the master asks, the outstation has to build its answer: scan its internal database of points, encode them into DNP3 objects, and hand the bytes to the network. That work takes time — the processing delay. Drag the slider in the panel above and watch the observer's readout move: a snappy software box answers in about 1 ms; a loaded legacy PLC with thousands of points can take tens of milliseconds.

On our real testbed the outstation answered in ≈1.01 ms and acknowledged in ≈0.24 ms. That 0.24 ms is itself a giveaway: it says "fast server-class Linux stack," not "embedded field RTU." Every layer of the timing tells the observer something.

Analogy: a librarian with a small shelf finds your book fast; one with a vast catalogue takes longer. You can estimate the size of the collection purely from the wait — without ever seeing the shelves.

Identify · the crown-jewel leak

Processing time is roughly proportional to database size — so timing leaks size.

Here's the leak that matters most. Because building the answer scales with how many points the device holds, the processing time rises with the database size. Plot one against the other and the points line up. An attacker who only has a stopwatch can read the device's size — and therefore guess its type and role — straight off the timing.

Attacker's view · database size vs response time

Right now the trend line is steep and tight: given a response time, the attacker reads off the size with confidence. Flip the defense and watch the cloud flatten — the timing stops depending on size, and the line goes flat. That flat line is the goal.

This is the single most important sentence on the page: the defense's real job is to break the link between timing and size — to make a small device and a large device take the same observable time. Hold that thought; it's what makes one defense cheap and another expensive.

Clarify · which "ACK" are we even talking about?

"ACK delay" is a slight misnomer — here there are only two clocks.

The acknowledgment stack · what's really on the wire

The twist: the acknowledgment and the answer usually arrive together

Our outstation piggybacks: for all 9 of 9 measured requests, the low-level TCP "got it" rode along on the very same packet as the DNP3 answer. So "ACK time" and "response time" collapse into one observable. Toggle it:

Piggyback vs split acknowledgment

Piggyback = one packet carries both "got it" and the answer, so the observer gets a single clean processing-time reading. The choice between piggyback and split is itself a fingerprint (software boxes piggyback; slow embedded devices ACK first, then answer).

Bottom line for design: the only timing knobs that physically exist here are (1) the response-plus-piggybacked-ACK, and (2) the application-level CONFIRM handshake on multi-frame answers. That's the whole surface.

Defend · reshape the distribution

Two philosophies: randomize to mislead, or normalize to blend in.

Now the fun part. An in-network element sits between the master and the outstation and delays the answer — never touching the bytes, only when they leave. The question is by how much, and according to what rule. Below is a whole population of responses. Pick a policy and watch two things: how identifiable the device stays (attacker) and how much latency you pay (cost).

Native: the observed times spread out exactly as processing time does — a clean fingerprint, zero cost.

Randomize (jitter) — RAINCOAT's family

Add a random pause each time. It smears a single reading, but the average still tracks the size — an observer who polls a few extra times averages the noise away and recovers the fingerprint. Cost buys you little. Good for misdirection about grid state; weak for hiding device identity.

Normalize (constant / decorrelate) — the wedge

Force every device to emit on the same schedule, so size no longer changes the observable time. Constant-time holds everything to a fixed deadline (airtight, but slowest). Decorrelate only removes the size dependence, not all variance — it kills the crown-jewel leak at a fraction of the latency. This "make everyone look alike" stance is what distinguishes the contribution from prior randomization work.

The takeaway the bench is built to show: killing the size-leak is cheap; flattening the entire distribution is expensive. "Size-decorrelate" is the sweet spot, and it's a result you can demonstrate with the one real device you already have — just vary its database size.

Budget · you can't stall forever

Delay is a resource with a hard ceiling — and overspending is itself a tell.

Holding a packet buys privacy but costs latency, and the network is watching the clock too. Counter-intuitively, the limit isn't a DNP3 timeout — those are all 5 to 60 seconds. It's TCP: if the master's transport layer doesn't hear back within its retransmit timeout (~200 ms), it assumes the packet was lost and re-sends. That retransmission is a visible twitch on the wire — the one thing both the eavesdropper and an intrusion-detection system will notice. So the safety limit and the stealth limit are the same number.

Delay budget · how long can you hold?
slider is logarithmic: 0 ms → 8 s
invisible & safe Well inside the TCP retransmit window. Session healthy, observer sees nothing unusual.

The current harness delays 10 ms per chunk — about 20× inside the safe zone. Multi-frame answers compound (each fragment waits on a CONFIRM), so the real per-fragment budget is the 5 s ceiling divided by the number of fragments. Rule of thumb: stay under ~200 ms and you're both correct and quiet.

Deploy · where the box lives

Where you hold the packet decides how hard it is.

"Delaying a packet" is trivial software but surprisingly awkward on a high-speed switch, because a switch ASIC has no "wait" instruction. Three homes, easiest first:

buildable now
Software replay server
The current split_server.py. It generates the answer bytes, so it just schedules when to send — no "hold a live packet" problem at all.
  • Every policy works here
  • Zero new hardware
  • Where the first result should be proven
medium
Tofino P4 switch
The real in-network target. Pacing (spacing frames out) is native and cheap; holding one frame for a precise delay isn't native — you fake it by recirculating the packet until a deadline.
  • Great for gap/rate normalization
  • Precise holds are a workaround
  • Cheap because DNP3 is tiny & slow
clean, needs HW
BlueField DPU
A smart NIC with real memory and timers. Holding to a deadline is straightforward, so constant-time normalization is exact here. Natural home to fuse splitting + timing into one box.
  • Exact constant-time
  • The "ground-truth" baseline
  • Gated on the purchase

The elegant framing hidden here: the recirculation trick that would melt a data-center switch is cheap for DNP3 precisely because grid traffic is small and infrequent — Dr. Lin's "don't go too deep on an ASIC" intuition, but quantified in our favor.

Your move · places to invent

Open questions — where your ideas go.

You now have the whole mental model. Here are the seams where a new idea would land — each is a lever from a panel above, turned into a research question:

target
What shape should "normal" be? Constant, uniform-random-within-budget, or shaped to impersonate a specific decoy device so the attacker misclassifies it as something harmless? Each is a different privacy meaning.
adaptive
What if the attacker knows the defense? A clever observer aggregates thousands of polls or attacks the residue you didn't flatten (the distribution's tail, the gaps between fragments, the CONFIRM latency). Which channel leaks last?
compose
Does timing + splitting beat either alone? Splitting reshapes shape, timing reshapes when. Do they stack into more privacy than the sum — and does normalizing the gaps between split chunks close a channel neither closes alone?
budget
Can you spend the budget adaptively? Poll cadence is seconds; the hold is milliseconds. Could the box learn each device's worst-case and set the deadline per-device, buying privacy without over-paying latency?
line
How far before it's a proxy? Pure delay is byte-preserving and in-scope. Forging an early ACK to decouple it from a held answer is powerful but crosses into proxy/MITM territory — a rule you'd have to consciously relax.

Reference · plain-language glossary

The terms, in one place.

Outstation
The field device (sensor/relay box). It holds a database of "points" and answers when the master asks.
Master
The control-center side that polls the outstation for readings.
Passive observer
An eavesdropper that only reads the wire — never injects, blocks, or edits packets.
Fingerprint
Stable, observable traits (size, frame shape, timing) that identify a device across sessions.
Processing time
How long the outstation takes to build its answer — the gap the observer times. Scales with database size.
Piggyback ACK
The TCP "got it" riding on the same packet as the DNP3 answer, so both arrive at once.
CONFIRM
A DNP3 application-level acknowledgment used to gate the next fragment of a multi-part answer.
Byte-preserving
Changing only when bytes leave, never which bytes — so the master reassembles the identical message.
Normalization
Reshaping timing so many devices look alike (blend into a crowd).
Randomization
Adding entropy to each reading to mislead — averaging can defeat it.
Decorrelation
Removing only the dependence of timing on size — the cheap way to kill the main leak.
TCP RTO
The transport retransmit timeout (~200 ms). Overshoot it and the master re-sends — a visible tell.
