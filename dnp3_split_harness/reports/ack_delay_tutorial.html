How a DNP3 device betrays itself in its ACK timing — and how to hide it.

A passive listener on a substation link can name the outstation without decoding one DNP3 byte, purely from when its TCP ACK and response appear. This page walks the whole study end to end: what the six real device captures show, how the ACK is delayed for devices that piggyback it versus devices that send it separately, the byte-preserving timing defense, and an honest before/after of device fingerprinting.

00The whole idea, in plain language

You don't need any networking knowledge to follow this. Everything on the page rests on seven simple ideas. Read these once — each has an everyday analogy — and the rest will make sense. If a term ever feels unfamiliar later, it is defined here or in the glossary at the end.

1

A power grid is constantly talking to itself

A utility's control room continuously checks on equipment out in the field — protective relays, meters, breakers sitting in substations. In this story the control room is called the master, and each field device is an outstation. The language they speak is a protocol called DNP3.

A dispatcher on the radio all day: “Unit 12, status?” — “Unit 12, all normal.” DNP3 is just the radio language, and it runs over the same internet plumbing (TCP) your web browser uses.
2

Messages travel as little numbered packets

Computers don't send a message in one lump. They chop it into small chunks called packets and number them, so the far end can reassemble them in order. The system that does this reliably is TCP.

Mailing a long letter as a stack of numbered postcards. The recipient sorts them back into order using the numbers.
3

Every packet gets a tiny receipt — the “ACK”

TCP is reliable because the receiver must confirm each packet arrived. It sends back a tiny note that means “got it” — called an ACK (short for acknowledgement). An ACK carries no real content; it is pure bookkeeping. Remember this word — the whole page is about the timing of these little receipts.

Certified mail's return receipt: a small card that only says “delivered,” nothing else.
📨 request  →  🏭 device  →  ✅ ACK “got it”
4

The master asks; the outstation answers

Two kinds of traffic matter here. The master sends a request (“give me your readings”). The outstation sends back a response (the actual readings). The request goes one way; the response comes back the other way.

A question and an answer. The eavesdropper below is timing the gap between them.
5

An eavesdropper can watch without reading

Now imagine someone quietly tapping the wire. Even if they can't read what's inside the packets, they can still see two things about every packet: how big it is, and exactly when it was sent. That's a passive eavesdropper — they only watch, never touch or alter anything.

Standing by a mailbox watching sealed envelopes go in and out. You can't read the letters, but you learn their size and the precise timing — and that alone turns out to be a lot.
6

Timing is a fingerprint

Every device model has timing habits: how quickly it fires the “got it” receipt, how long it takes to produce an answer, whether it sends the receipt separately or bundled with the answer. Those habits are consistent enough to identify the exact device model — a fingerprint — without ever seeing the contents.

Recognizing a friend from across the street just by their walk. Machines have a “walk” too, made of packet sizes and timing.
7

Why identifying the device is dangerous — and what we do about it

For someone planning to disrupt the grid, the first step is reconnaissance: figuring out what equipment is where. Learning “that box is a SEL-751 protection relay” hands the attacker the model's known weaknesses and hints at how the substation is wired — all without breaking any encryption. Our goal is to erase that timing fingerprint while never changing a single byte of the real DNP3 message (the readings stay exactly correct).

Same building, but now everyone leaves at random staggered times and takes identical taxis — an observer outside can no longer tell who is who, even though everyone still gets home with the same belongings.
the fix, in one sentence

Make every device reveal its answer on the same schedule, so a watcher can't tell the naturally-fast devices from the slow ones — while never altering the answer itself. The rest of this page shows exactly how, proves it works on real captured traffic, and is honest about what it does not yet hide.

With those seven ideas in hand, here is the threat in detail.

The threat

01What a passive listener actually sees

The attacker never breaks encryption or injects packets. They just tap the link and read TCP/IP headers — packet sizes, and the timestamps of the request, the ACK, and the response. From those alone, three quantities leak the device.

🔰 in plain terms

The four tiles below are just four things a wire-watcher can measure with a stopwatch and a ruler, no decryption needed. Think of the request as “the question was asked” and the response as “the answer arrived.” req→ACK = how long until the device says “got your question.” ACK→resp gap = the pause between “got it” and the actual answer — essentially how long the device spent thinking. req→resp = total time from question to answer. resp size = how big the answer packet is. Different device models have different, consistent values for these — that's the leak. (“Formby-style” below just credits the researchers who first showed grid devices can be fingerprinted this way.)

Why the ACK matters

On a link where a device sends a pure TCP ACK first and the DNP3 response later, the attacker estimates processing time as apparent = response_timestamp − ack_timestamp. That gap is a Formby-style fingerprint. Our whole Phase-2 goal is to manipulate that observer-visible gap without ever changing the device's true processing time.

The core concept

02Combined ACK vs Separate ACK

Whether the TCP ACK travels together with the DNP3 response or before it is a host-kernel behaviour, not a DNP3 rule. This single difference is the biggest device tell in the whole study. Watch both exchanges play out:

🔰 in plain terms

Recall the “got it” receipt (the ACK) from idea 3. When a device receives a request, its operating system has a choice about that receipt. It can staple the receipt onto the answer and send them as one packet — that's combined. Or it can send the bare receipt first, then the answer a moment later — that's separate. Why would it ever split them? Because the OS briefly waits, hoping the answer is about to be ready so it can save a packet by stapling; if the answer is slow, it gives up and sends the receipt alone. Different device models make this choice differently, and that choice is visible to the eavesdropper — so it is itself a fingerprint.

The mechanism

04The socket program — how the ACK is delayed

To manipulate the ACK without forging a single packet, we exploit the Linux delayed-ACK timer. When the outstation receives a request, the kernel wants to ACK it. Whether that ACK rides with the response or comes out alone depends entirely on how long the application waits before it write()s the response.

🔰 in plain terms

From idea 2, that “brief wait before stapling the receipt” isn't magic — the operating system has an actual built-in stopwatch for it, roughly 40 milliseconds on Linux. Here's the trick: we never fake a packet. We just tell the device software to wait a little longer before it hands over its answer. If it answers within 40 ms, the receipt rides along (combined). If we make it wait past 40 ms, the OS's stopwatch runs out, it gives up and fires the bare receipt on its own (separate), and the answer follows. So by choosing how long to hold the answer, we control which of the two behaviours the world sees — using the OS's own rules, forging nothing. (“write()” below just means “the program hands its answer to the network.”)

The experiment (ack_separation_probe.py)

A minimal TCP client/server — no DNP3 stack, no forging. The server records arrival with time.monotonic_ns(), sleeps a controlled delay, then sends a fixed 2407 B response (same size/segment count as the real DNP3 read). A privileged capture on the server's egress is the ground truth for whether a pure ACK appeared. If it can't capture, it records unknown — it never guesses.

The defense

05The timing policy — normalize, don't jitter

All logic lives in one reusable module, timing_policy.py (22/22 unit tests). The scheduler is a pure function of timestamps; the caller does an absolute-deadline wait before any byte leaves. Bytes are never touched, so b"".join(chunks) == response always holds. Native mode waits zero and is wire-identical.

🔰 in plain terms

Picture a quiz where fast contestants blurt answers instantly and slow ones take a few seconds — you could tell them apart by speed alone. Now impose one rule: everyone must wait until the 25-second mark to answer, no matter how fast they actually knew it. Suddenly they all look equally fast; the speed tell is gone. That is exactly what we do to the device's response timing — hold the finished answer until a fixed target moment, so every device releases on the same schedule. A tempting but wrong fix is to add a random pause to each answer, because “your real speed + a random bit” still leaks your real speed on average. The right fix (“normalization”) targets the same release time for everyone. And “bytes are never touched” means we only change when the answer appears, never what it says — the readings stay perfectly correct.

✗ Wrong — additive jitter

visible = native_time + random_jitter
# still leaks: fast device stays fast

✓ Right — bounded normalization

target  = sample(class-independent dist)
desired = request_arrival + target
release = max(response_ready, desired)

The target never depends on CROB count, response/request size, native ready time, or device identity (unit-tested). Two devices that meet the target look identical on the wire.

Send immediately + log a bypass if: deadline already missed, target > RTO-safe bound, queue over limit, transaction critical, or traffic unsupported.

Measured RTO bound

Not a guessed 200 ms — rto_probe.py measured RTO floor ≈ 211 ms (Linux TCP_RTO_MIN); safe hold ≈ 105 ms. The 10–25 ms targets sit far under it.

rig-validated

A real pydnp3 master completes a full integrity poll with byte-preservation PASS and no DNP3 timeout.

The one rule that makes it a defense, not jitter: the target is sampled from a class-independent distribution, and the response is released at max(response_ready, request_arrival + target) — so a fast device and a slow device that both meet the target look identical. Where timing enters the live server: one call in serve_once, right before the bytes are written. It stamps request-arrival, asks the scheduler for a release deadline, and does an absolute-deadline wait (recomputed each loop so jitter never accumulates). In native mode scheduler is None → it returns immediately and the wire is byte-for-byte unchanged.
# split_server.py — the whole timing hook (bytes are never touched here)
def _apply_timing(self, flow_id, request, parsed, entry, request_received_ns):
    if self.scheduler is None:
        return None                                  # native → wire-identical
    response_ready_ns = time.monotonic_ns()
    decision = self.scheduler.decide(
        flow_id=flow_id, transaction_id=self._request_count,
        request_received_ns=request_received_ns,
        response_ready_ns=response_ready_ns,
        request_size=len(request),
        response_size=len(entry["response_bytes"]),
        is_critical=self._is_critical(parsed),        # control fc → bypass
        rto_safe_ms=self.rto_safe_ms)
    tpol.wait_until(decision.actual_release_ns)        # absolute-deadline block
    return decision

Phase-2 reschedules two packets that already exist — it never creates one. The ordering invariant ack_release ≤ response_release is enforced by a final clamp, so a delayed ACK is never emitted after the response. gap-normalized places the ACK exactly the target gap before the response.
# timing_policy.py — plan_ack_response_release() : reschedule an EXISTING pure ACK + response
if   ack_mode == "ack-delay-only":      ack_rel  = ack_ready_ns + ack_delay_ns      # gap shrinks
elif ack_mode == "response-delay-only": resp_rel = response_ready_ns + response_delay_ns  # gap grows
elif ack_mode == "independent-delay":  ack_rel += ack_delay_ns; resp_rel += response_delay_ns
elif ack_mode == "gap-normalized":
    resp_rel = max(response_ready_ns, ack_ready_ns + gap_target_ns)
    ack_rel  = max(ack_ready_ns, resp_rel - gap_target_ns)   # pin gap to a constant

if ack_rel > resp_rel:                         # ordering invariant: ACK must not follow response
    ack_rel = resp_rel; clamped = True
return AckResponsePlan(ack_rel, resp_rel, gap_ns=resp_rel - ack_rel, ...)

The attacker model, in code. Four feature families; the defense transform touches only timing (Phase-1 hold + Phase-2 gap-norm), never the bytes. The plus_ackmode what-if additionally rewrites the ACK mode to expose the residual. Capture-level split (train base PCAP, test the disjoint L PCAP) prevents adjacent packets leaking across the split.
# ack_fingerprint_eval.py — feature families + the defense transform
FEATURES = {"ack_only":["is_separate","req_to_ack_ms","ack_to_resp_ms"],
            "timing":["req_to_resp_ms"], "size":["req_size","resp_size"], "all":[...]}

def apply_defense(x, scenario):
    x["req_to_resp_ms"] = np.maximum(x["req_to_resp_ms"], 25)   # Phase-1 hold
    sep = x["is_separate"] == 1
    x.loc[sep, "ack_to_resp_ms"] = 20                          # Phase-2 gap-norm
    if scenario == "plus_ackmode":                          # WHAT-IF: also hide ACK mode
        x["is_separate"] = 0; x["ack_to_resp_ms"] = 0; x["req_to_resp_ms"] = 25
    return x

The attacker, before vs after

08Fingerprinting & clustering the devices

ack_fingerprint_eval.py — 11,494 transactions, capture-level split (train each base PCAP → test its disjoint L PCAP). Supervised classifiers + unsupervised clustering (scored by Adjusted Rand Index against the true device). Three scenarios: native (before) · timing_gap_norm (the implemented defense) · plus_ackmode (a hypothetical what-if — not the shipped defense — that also erases the ACK-style tell, included to reveal the leftover leakage; fully explained just below the toggle).

🔰 in plain terms — how to read the two tables

We put ourselves in the attacker's shoes and try to name the device from its timing. Two ways: (1) Supervised “accuracy” — we train a guesser on labelled examples, then test it: accuracy is the fraction of transactions it labels with the correct device. There are 3 devices and blind guessing scores about 0.40, so anything well above 0.40 means the fingerprint is real and leaking. (2) Unsupervised “ARI” — we hand a clustering algorithm the transactions with no labels at all and let it group ones that “look alike”; ARI measures how well those automatic groups line up with the true devices: 1.0 = perfect match, 0.0 = no better than random. The four “feature families” in each table say which clues the attacker is allowed to use: ack_only (just the ACK behaviour), timing (just the round-trip time), size (just packet sizes), or all (everything). 🔰 in plain terms — what the three buttons mean

BEFORE — native: the raw devices with no defense at all. This is the attacker's baseline.

AFTER — implemented: the devices with the defense we actually built and tested switched on. It normalizes the response timing and the ACK-to-response gap. This is real, running, byte-preserving code.

AFTER — what-if: a hypothetical “what would happen if…” experiment — not a real defense. We take the recorded data and, on paper, additionally erase the last timing tell: we make every device also use the same ACK style (all “combined,” so no device gives itself away by sending a separate receipt). We do not actually do this in the shipped defense — we simulate it purely to answer one question: “if we could also hide the ACK style, would the fingerprint finally vanish?” The answer (below) is revealing: ACK-based identification drops all the way to random guessing — but the device is still not fully hidden, because its packet size still leaks. So the what-if button is there to show you both the ceiling of what an ACK-focused defense can achieve and the leftover leak (size) that would need a different tool (byte-preserving padding). It is labelled “what-if” precisely because it is a thought experiment about a future primitive, not something the current code performs.

the key, non-obvious finding

Normalizing the ACK gap does NOT defeat ACK fingerprinting. Under the implemented defense, ACK-only accuracy is unchanged (0.810 → 0.810) and clustering barely moves — because a separate ACK still exists; pinning its gap to a constant does not make SEL-751 look combined. 09What is proven — and what is not

Proven / validated

  • The trace fingerprint is real (native all-features device-ID 0.888–0.897 ≫ chance 0.400), riding mainly on size and ACK mode.
  • Byte-preserving, class-independent normalization: 22/22 unit tests, rig-validated 930 txns, wire-pinned, 0 miss/bypass/reset.
  • Delaying the write naturally induces a pure ACK at the 40 ms delayed-ACK threshold — no forging (1,808 txns, 0 resets).
  • Binding safety bound (TCP RTO ≈ 211 ms) is measured; targets sit far under it.

Explicitly NOT claimed

  • Normalization does not remove all leakage — size and ACK mode survive it.
  • ACK manipulation changes only the observer-visible gap, never true processing time.
  • The 40 ms threshold & SEL-751 profile are measured on this stack/trace/host; they don't auto-generalize.
  • The before/after is a simulation on measured timings, not a live capture; P4 can hold packets but not safely synthesize a TCP ACK (deferred).

10Terminology (used strictly)

pure TCP ACK
A zero-payload TCP acknowledgement packet, on its own.
ACK-bearing DNP3 response
A DNP3 response segment that also acknowledges (the ACK piggybacks). Never called an "application ACK."
DNP3 application CONFIRM
Reserved for an actual DNP3 CONFIRM function code — none appeared in these traces.
ACK→response gap
response_release − ack_release; the observer-visible readout an attacker uses as apparent processing time.
combined / separate
Whether the ACK travels with the response (combined) or as a pure ACK before it (separate) — a host-kernel behaviour.
bounded normalization
Hold each response to a class-independent target = max(ready, arrival+target); not native+jitter.
fail-open
On any safety trigger, send immediately and log a bypass — never hold unsafely.
