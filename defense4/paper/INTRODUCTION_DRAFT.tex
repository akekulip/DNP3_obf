\section{Introduction}

An attacker studies a target before acting on it. Fingerprinting is how a passive observer turns
raw network traffic into knowledge about the systems that produce it, learning a device's type,
its role, its vendor, or its behavior without ever decoding the payload~\cite{kohno2005remote,
radhakrishnan2014gtid, shu2006fingerprint}. This knowledge is the reconnaissance stage of an
intrusion: it tells the attacker which hosts matter and how they will respond. The concern is
acute for industrial control systems and the power grid, where the devices are long-lived, their
roles are stable, and prior work has fingerprinted supervisory and field equipment directly from
its traffic~\cite{jeon2016passive, formby2016control}. One such feature is the cross-layer response
time, the delay between a request and the device's reply, which Formby et al. showed distinguishes
control-system devices by vendor and model~\cite{formby2016control}. Because a legacy protocol such
as DNP3 carries this timing in the clear, an on-path observer can read the fingerprint from ordinary
polling traffic.

Traffic obfuscation defends against such analysis by shaping what the observer sees. Existing work
shapes packet size, inter-packet timing, sending rate, or the overall communication
pattern~\cite{wright2009morphing, cai2014csbuflo, juarez2016wtfpad, apthorpe2019stp}, and stronger
analyses have repeatedly defeated the weaker of these countermeasures~\cite{dyer2012peekaboo,
sirinam2018df}. Many of these defenses were designed for the Internet setting, where the traffic is
already encrypted or otherwise payload-opaque. There, the shaper is free to add cover packets or reshape
flows without contradicting anything the observer can read~\cite{juarez2016wtfpad, wright2009morphing}.
Legacy industrial traffic breaks that assumption. DNP3 exchanges are plaintext and each application
block carries a validating checksum, so an observer can tell a genuine protocol exchange from an
injected dummy or an altered response~\cite{east2009taxonomy, fovino2010modbus}. A defense that
transforms this traffic therefore cannot rely on cover payloads or byte edits, and the techniques
built for encrypted flows do not transfer directly.

The result is a gap. The field devices are vendor-supplied and cannot be reprogrammed in practice, and the
deployed protocol cannot be changed~\cite{east2009taxonomy, cardenas2011attacks}, so a practical
defense must sit in the network and remain transparent to both endpoints. Programmable switches make
such in-network shaping feasible at line rate~\cite{wang2020pinot, meier2022ditto, kfoury2021p4survey},
but a defense for plaintext DNP3 must satisfy constraints those general shapers do not. It must
preserve the two endpoints, the DNP3 request and response exchange, the original packet bytes, and
the correctness of every delivered reply. It must also keep any added delay inside the DNP3, TCP,
polling, and quality-of-service limits so the control loop still functions. We assume the switch is a
trusted observation and control point on the plaintext path, and that the adversary is a passive
observer on the master-facing segment, where only the released, shaped timing is visible. Within these constraints, the response-time fingerprint remains open,
because the size-oriented and encrypted-flow defenses above do not address the request-to-reply timing
of a legacy device that cannot be modified.

We present an in-network framework that conceals the response-time fingerprint of a
separate-acknowledgment DNP3 device without touching the device, the protocol, or the packet contents. A single programmable
switch sits between the DNP3 master and the field device and observes the events of each polling
transaction. Rather than editing any packet, it controls when the device's original acknowledgment
and response become externally visible. The original packets stay resident in the switch's queues
while small internal marker packets recirculate to hold their place. The traffic manager then releases
the held packets on a schedule the control plane sets. This design works with, rather than against,
the fact that a data-plane pipeline cannot recall an already-enqueued packet at an arbitrary later
time chosen in software. Configurable gates on the acknowledgment and on the response give several
transformations of the observed timing: an event-driven mode that releases the acknowledgment only
when the matching response appears, an acknowledgment-deadline mode, and a dual-deadline mode that
holds both. These are modes of one framework rather than separate systems, and the control plane
selects among them and sets the delays at run time.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.} This paper makes the following contributions, each bounded by the
evidence we report.
\begin{itemize}
\item We frame the cross-layer response time, identified by Formby et al.~\cite{formby2016control} as a
device-fingerprinting feature, as a reachable in-network shaping target for legacy DNP3 devices, and
we frame concealing it as an in-network shaping problem under the plaintext, byte-preserving constraints
of the setting.
\item We design a unified, queue-resident timing framework that controls the visibility of the
original acknowledgment and response through traffic-manager scheduling, and we show how the
event-driven, acknowledgment-deadline, response-deadline, and dual-deadline transformations arise as
configurations of one mechanism. A separate-acknowledgment device releases its acknowledgment before
its response, so the response obligation must survive the acknowledgment release; we show how the
transaction state is preserved across that release so a later response is still held rather than
forwarded unshaped.
\item We resolve the DNP3, TCP, and Tofino constraints the design meets: matching each transaction to
its DNP3 transaction-generation code on plaintext traffic, preserving the original bytes, keeping the internal markers
off the observable wire, and fitting the arithmetic within a single Tofino-1 ingress pipeline.
\item We implement the framework on an Intel Tofino-1 switch and evaluate it on silicon against a
physical SEL-751 relay. The response-deadline and dual-deadline modes normalize the measured response
time from a variable native distribution to a fixed value, the acknowledgment and response order is
preserved, no original packet is dropped or altered, and the runtime fail-open path releases held
packets within a bounded time and re-arms the next transaction. We report these results, and their
limits, for one relay, for READ polling transactions, and for the response-time observable only; we do
not claim device indistinguishability, size concealment, or a general multi-flow defense.
\end{itemize}
