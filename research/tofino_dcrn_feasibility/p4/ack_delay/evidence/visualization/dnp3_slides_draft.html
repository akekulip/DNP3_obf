02 / 08
The problem & the two designs

Response time leaks the device — two ways to erase it

The gap between a device's low-level acknowledgment and its actual answer is a stable, device-specific number. Prior work (Formby, NDSS 2016) fingerprints devices from exactly this. Both of our defenses hold one frame briefly on the switch and forward everything unchanged.

Case A — reduce

Hold the acknowledgment

Keep the quick ACK on the switch and let it out right when the answer arrives, so the observed gap shrinks to a tiny fixed guard.

gap → δ  (≈ 0)
Case B — increase

Hold the answer

Forward the ACK immediately, then release the answer at a common deadline measured from that ACK, so every device shows the same gap.

tout = max( tready,  tACK + G )

Both are ACK-anchored, byte-preserving, and event- or deadline-governed — not the old request-relative delay. The result of either is a device-independent gap.

04 / 08
First attempt — software

We started on the host with eBPF timing

The natural first move was to schedule departures in the kernel (eBPF Earliest-Departure-Time). It proved the timing idea on the wire, but ran into limits that pushed us into the network itself.

  • Host-only. The delay lives on one machine's stack, not on the shared path a real device sits behind.
  • Privilege & fragility. Loading the programs needs elevated rights that are locked down on the rig.
  • Can't hold-then-decide. As an egress drop, the answer has already left before the logic can choose to delay it — and the kernel's quick-ACK can't retroactively delay an ACK that already went out.

Conclusion: do the holding in the network, on a device that sits on the path and can genuinely pause a frame.

05 / 08
The build — on the switch

Replay real devices over TCP, delay on the Tofino

Authentic traffic

We drive genuine recorded device request/response bytes over a live TCP connection through the switch, reproducing each device's real response-time distribution — not synthetic timing.

Hold in hardware

The switch parks the chosen frame in a recirculation loop and releases it on the response event (Case A) or at a wall-clock deadline (Case B). No header, sequence, or checksum is ever rewritten.

Rig: one host plays both master and outstation through the switch's loopback; the defense runs entirely in the data plane. Poll rate is ~1 per 2 s, so millisecond holds are comfortably affordable.

After Case A the two devices sit on top of each other near zero; after Case B they sit on top of each other at a shared constant. Either way the two groups become one — byte-identical, no retransmissions. What remains (whether an ACK is separate, and the answer's size) is out of timing's scope.

The 12-stage wall. Case A already uses all 12 stages. Adding size-padding (which rewrites TCP sequence numbers and needs a checksum) and segmentation/split — each wants its own stages and stateful units — will not co-reside with timing in one Tofino-1 pipeline. Timing, size, and split together exceed a single switch pipeline.

Next — more room to work

A SmartNIC on Vision to lift the ceiling

To host timing and size and split together — and to get a real programmable timer instead of a recirculation trick — we're evaluating a Netronome SmartNIC on the Vision host as an alternative data plane for the testbed.

  • More cores and memory than a fixed 12-stage pipeline, with an on-NIC notion of time.
  • Sits on Vision's path, same role the switch plays now — the master↔device flow traverses the NIC.
  • Open question we're researching: programming model, a native delay/timer primitive, and current availability.
