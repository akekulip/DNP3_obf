The problem & the two designs

On separate-ACK devices (Case A, e.g. SEL-751): two defenses erase it

  • The gap between a device's quick acknowledgment and its real answer is a stable, device-specific number (Formby, NDSS 2016).
Defense 1 — reduce gap

Hold the acknowledgment

  • Keep the ACK on the switch
  • Release it when the answer arrives
  • Gap shrinks to ~0
Defense 2 — increase gap

Hold the answer

  • Forward the ACK immediately
  • Release the answer at a common deadline
  • Gap becomes a fixed constant
  • Either way, the gap stops depending on the device.

First attempt — software

We started on the host with eBPF timing

  • Scheduled packet departures in the kernel (eBPF earliest-departure-time). Proved the idea on the wire.
  • Host-only — the delay lives on one machine, not on the shared path a real device sits behind.
  • Needs privilege — loading the programs requires rights locked down on the rig.
  • Can't hold-then-decide — as an egress drop the answer has already left; the kernel can't take back an ACK it already sent.
  • Conclusion: do the holding in the network, on a device that sits on the path and can truly pause a frame.
05 / 13
The build — on the switch

Replay real devices over TCP, delay on the Tofino

  • Drive genuine recorded device bytes over a live TCP connection through the switch.
  • Reproduce each device's real response-time distribution — not synthetic timing.
  • Park the chosen frame in a recirculation loop and release it on the response (A) or at a deadline (B).
  • No header, sequence number, or checksum is ever rewritten — byte-identical.
  • One host plays both master and device through the switch's loopback; the defense runs entirely in the data plane.
06 / 13
Delay policy & mechanism

The rule we enforce — and how the switch holds a frame

The delay policy

  • Defense 1 (hold the ACK): release the ACK the moment the response is ready → gap → ~0. Event-governed.
  • Defense 2 (hold the response): forward the ACK at once; release the response at ACK time + G → gap = G, the same for every device. Deadline-governed.
  • Rule (B): send at max(ready, tACK + G); the target G is loaded at runtime, not compiled in.
  • Two modes: FIXED (one target G) and BOUNDED (a common band) — both independent of device, size, and native timing.

The hold mechanism

  • Per-flow state machine: arm on the request → match the exact pure ACK → hold the chosen frame → release → clear.
  • Hold = park the frame in the switch's recirculation loop and re-check each pass; release through the traffic-manager shaper.
  • Byte-preserving: the frame is only delayed — no header, sequence number, or checksum is rewritten.
  • Fail-open: if the deadline can't be met, forward — never drop.

Why not the switch

The switch proved timing — but can't hold all three

  • Both timing defenses are proven on the switch — that result stands.
  • The switch has a fixed pipeline of 12 processing stages. Defense 1 already fills all 12.
  • Size-padding rewrites sequence numbers and the checksum — it needs its own stages. Split needs its own stages too.
  • The switch has no real timer — the millisecond hold is a recirculation workaround that added a ~47 ms timing artifact we had to measure and work around.

Why the SmartNIC

The Netronome runs the datapath as ordinary code

  • No fixed 12-stage wall — it is a general processor with ~60 cores and gigabytes of memory.
  • Timing, size, and split become sequential logic sharing per-flow state — all three co-reside.
  • The millisecond hold is natural: park the frame in memory, release it from a timer thread. No recirculation, no ~47 ms artifact.
  • Confirmed on our bench: an Agilio 2×40G card is already in Vision, cabled to Hulk, link up at 10 G, 0.3 ms round-trip.
  • One dependency: the vendor's programming kit to build the on-card datapath. Until it arrives, the same logic runs on the Vision host over the same link.
